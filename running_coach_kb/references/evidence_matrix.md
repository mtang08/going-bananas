# Evidence Matrix

**Search date:** 2026-11-10  
**Scope:** Targeted critical review, not an exhaustive systematic search. Certainty describes confidence in the research finding; applicability separately addresses this athlete. Evidence IDs are stable within package version 1.0.0.

| Evidence ID | Question | Finding | Study population/design | Effect/uncertainty | Certainty | Applicability | Limitations | Source IDs |

| E-AER-01 | Aerobic progression | A 13-week program framed around a 10% weekly increase did not reduce injury versus an 8-week standard novice program. | RCT; 532 novice runners | Injury: 20.8% vs 20.3%; no meaningful difference. | Moderate | Directly challenges adoption of the 10% rule; population less trained than this athlete. | One program comparison cannot prove all rates equivalent. | S001 |
| E-AER-02 | Aerobic progression | Prospective running studies do not consistently identify a universal safe weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity, or progression threshold. | Systematic review; 36 studies, 23,047 runners | Conflicting associations; heterogeneous injury definitions and exposure methods. | Moderate | High for rejecting rigid universal gates; low for selecting this athlete’s exact progression. | Mostly observational; confounding and reverse causation. | S002 |
| E-AER-03 | Aerobic progression | A run substantially longer than the runner’s recent longest run was associated with higher overuse-injury rates, while weekly ACWR and week-to-week ratios were not useful in the same cohort. | Prospective wearable cohort; 5,205 runners | Single-session categories >10% above recent longest run: adjusted HRR 1.52-2.28; observational. | Moderate for association; low for cutoff causality | Useful as a novelty flag, not a safe/unsafe boundary or PTT rule. | Self-reported injury; distance only; threshold chosen by investigators; residual confounding. | S003 |
| E-AER-04 | Aerobic progression | ACWR should not be used as a causal injury-prevention rule or “sweet spot.” | Methodological critique plus 2026 cohort | Ratio artifacts; inconsistent direction; 2026 duration HR 1.33 disappeared when baseline-injured runners excluded. | Moderate | High for app design: display descriptively, never gate training automatically. | Does not mean recent load is irrelevant; better models may emerge. | S004; S005 |
| E-AER-05 | Return after illness | Return should be symptom- and severity-led, beginning with a controlled exercise challenge only after mild illness is very mild/resolved; monitor during, immediately after, and for 24 h. | IOC consensus informed by reviews | Consensus suggests 10-20 min at roughly 60-70% of normal intensity for selected mild cases. | Moderate clinical guidance | Useful after uncomplicated respiratory illness; not for cardiopulmonary or systemic symptoms. | Consensus threshold, not an RCT-derived universal rule. | S006; S007 |
| E-AER-06 | Return after illness | Illness severity and pathogen affect time to return; return to first training is not the same as full training or full performance. | Prospective cohort; 114 confirmed infections | Median 3.5 d to training, 8 d to full training, 11 d to full performance; wide individual variation. | Low-moderate | Supports staged rebuild and discourages immediate restoration of prior load. | Athletic cohort; not a prescription for this athlete. | S008 |
| E-INT-01 | Intensity distribution | Polarized and between-threshold programs both improved recreational runners; the primary between-group performance difference was not significant. | 10-week randomized trial; 30 recreational runners | 10 km change about 5.0% vs 3.6%; between-group nonsignificant. | Low-moderate | Direct population relevance but small sample. | One protocol and short intervention. | S009 |
| E-INT-02 | Intensity distribution | No overall polarized-versus-pyramidal winner is established; recreational athletes may respond at least as well to pyramidal training. | IPD network meta-analysis; 13 studies, 348 athletes | POL vs PYR: VO2max SMD -0.06; time trial SMD -0.05; both nonsignificant. | Moderate | High for rejecting mandatory 80/20 or polarization at the agreed rolling-cycle run count. | Mostly male; varied sports and zone methods. | S010 |
| E-INT-03 | Intensity distribution | A separate meta-analysis found a small VO2peak advantage for polarized training but no clear time-trial, threshold, or time-to-exhaustion advantage. | Meta-analysis; 17 studies, 437 athletes | VO2peak SMD 0.24 [0.01, 0.48]; other performance outcomes not clearly different. | Moderate | Suggests polarization is an option, not default superiority. | Short studies; trained populations; intervention heterogeneity. | S011 |
| E-INT-04 | Intensity definitions | Session-goal, time-in-zone, pace, HR, and RPE methods can classify the same training differently; two-, three-, and five-zone labels are not interchangeable. | Observational/methods studies | Substantial classification differences; no single conversion is valid. | High | Critical for dashboard: store raw anchors and method, not just a zone label. | Methods studies do not choose the best training distribution. | S012; S013 |
| E-INT-05 | Zone 2 | “Zone 2” lacks a universal historical definition; an expert panel favored work immediately below the first lactate/ventilatory threshold and noted adaptations are not unique to that narrow intensity. | Expert viewpoint | No comparative effect estimate. | Low-moderate | Useful terminology guardrail; not a precise prescription without measured thresholds. | Expert consensus, not outcome trial. | S014 |
| E-INT-06 | Norwegian threshold methods | Norwegian single/double-threshold models are primarily descriptive elite practice; an acute split-session study does not prove long-term superiority. | Qualitative elite best-practice study; acute crossover in 14 elite men | Splitting 6x10 min lowered some acute drift/fatigue markers with equal work. | Low | Low current applicability at low volume with lifting and PTT constraints. | Elite dose, lactate control, support systems, and recovery cannot be imported. | S015; S016 |
| E-INT-07 | Interval methods | Multiple interval structures improve fitness; longer multi-minute intervals may provide a strong VO2 stimulus, while very short formats can be lower perceived effort. | RCTs in recreational and well-trained runners; network meta-analysis | 4x4 min improved VO2max 6.5% in one high-dose trial; no universal format winner across meta-analysis. | Low-moderate | Supports later rotation by phase; does not justify frequent hard sessions now. | Trials often used 2-3 hard sessions/week and healthier tendons. | S017; S018; S019 |
| E-INT-08 | Minimal speed exposure | Direct evidence for relaxed strides or short hills as a minimum effective speed dose in tendon-limited recreational runners is insufficient. | Evidence gap; inference from interval and neuromuscular principles | No validated dose or injury benefit. | Insufficient | May be used later as calibrated skill/speed exposure only if symptoms tolerate it. | Do not equate relaxed strides with all-out sprint intervals. | S017; S019 |
| E-LONG-01 | Long-run development | Higher weekly volume and longer endurance runs are associated with better half-marathon/marathon performance, but causality and safe individual doses are not established. | Prospective observational cohort and meta-regression | Performance associations; no injury association in one 997-runner cohort. | Moderate for association; low for prescription | Supports gradual long-run development over years, not a premature marathon block. | Self-selection, self-report, healthy-runner bias. | S020; S021 |
| E-LONG-02 | Long-run caps | Popular rules limiting the long run to a fixed percentage of weekly mileage are coaching conventions, not directly validated injury-prevention thresholds. | No direct validating trial located | No supported universal percentage. | Insufficient | Use duration, recent longest run, recovery, and delayed tendon response instead. | Absence of evidence does not prove caps are useless as heuristics. | S002; S003; S020 |
| E-LONG-03 | Cross-training | Cycling can preserve some aerobic fitness when running load is constrained, but evidence does not prove one-for-one interchangeability or preserve running-specific tissue tolerance. | Small RCT; 2026 meta-analysis of 7 RCTs | No clear between-group differences; wide CIs; 11-runner study maintained estimated VO2max over 5 weeks. | Low-moderate | Useful substitution/addition when PTT load is limiting, with mode-specific calibration. | Short, heterogeneous, often old protocols. | S022; S023 |
| E-HYB-01 | Concurrent training | Concurrent endurance plus resistance training generally preserves hypertrophy and maximal-strength gains versus resistance alone; explosive-strength gains can be attenuated, especially same-session. | Meta-analysis; 43 studies | Max strength SMD -0.06; hypertrophy -0.01; explosive strength -0.28. | Moderate | Strong support for preserving meaningful twice-weekly lifting. | Few studies match this exact running/lifting schedule; trained subgroup uncertainty. | S024 |
| E-HYB-02 | Strength for running | High-load, plyometric, and combined strength methods can improve running economy. | Meta-analysis; 31 studies, 652 runners | High-load ES -0.266; combined -0.426; plyometric at slower speeds -0.307. | Moderate-low | Supports strength as complementary; plyometrics require PTT readiness. | Economy is not the same as race performance or injury reduction. | S025 |
| E-HYB-03 | Injury prevention | Exercise programs have not shown a clear pooled reduction in running injuries; one supervised hip/core trial in novices was favorable. | Meta-analysis plus 325-person RCT | Pooled injury risk/rate nonsignificant; Run RCT hip/core HR 0.66 [0.45, 0.97]. | Low-moderate | Do not market strength or mobility as injury insurance. | Programs, adherence, supervision, and populations vary. | S026; S027 |
| E-HYB-04 | Scheduling | Separating concurrent sessions by at least several hours reduced evidence of explosive-strength interference; exact soreness and key-session spacing must be individualized. | Meta-analysis subgroup | Same-session attenuation was larger; >=3 h subgroup not significant. | Low-moderate | Supports separation when explosive strength or run quality matters; not a mandatory 24/48-h rule. | Subgroup analysis; not an injury trial. | S024 |
| E-PTT-01 | PTT management | PTT-specific exercise evidence is sparse and mainly involves middle-aged adults with diagnosed stage I/II dysfunction plus orthoses/stretching. | Systematic review; 3 trials, 93 participants | Moderate effects reported for eccentric exercise plus co-interventions; dose poorly reported. | Low | Applies only as general support for progressive loading, not diagnosis or exact rehab. | Population and disease stage differ from an unassessed runner. | S028; S029; S030 |
| E-PTT-02 | PTT loading dose | No optimal PTT loading mode, intensity, frequency, or return-to-running criterion is established. | PTT systematic review and two small RCTs | All or both groups often improved; between-group differences inconsistent. | Low | Numeric rehab thresholds should remain null unless clinician-supplied or athlete-calibrated. | Sparse trials; orthotic co-interventions; no running-return trials. | S028; S029; S030 |
| E-PTT-03 | Tendon extrapolation | Broader tendinopathy evidence favors adequately loaded, not necessarily daily resistance, but the meta-analysis excluded PTT. | Meta-analysis; 110 studies, five other tendon sites | Higher external load and lower frequency showed favorable pooled patterns. | Moderate for included tendons; low for PTT | Can inform questions for a clinician, not an automatic PTT prescription. | Anatomic and functional transfer uncertain. | S031 |
| E-PTT-04 | Diagnosis boundary | Posterior tibial tendon symptoms, diagnosed PTT tendinopathy/dysfunction, and progressive collapsing foot deformity are not synonyms. | Clinical trials and expert nomenclature consensus | No effect estimate. | High for terminology | App must not diagnose or infer deformity from flat feet or medial ankle tenderness alone. | Clinical examination and sometimes imaging determine diagnosis/stage. | S028; S032 |
| E-PTT-05 | Orthoses and foot exercises | Orthoses may reduce pain in diagnosed early-stage PTTD; intrinsic-foot exercise can change surrogate foot measures, but injury and PTT return-to-run benefits are unproven. | Orthosis systematic review; intrinsic-foot systematic review | Limited, heterogeneous effects; no validated PTT prevention outcome. | Low | Optional clinician-guided load modifier; short-foot can remain if comfortable without being treated as curative. | Mixed populations; surrogate outcomes; high risk of bias. | S033; S036 |
| E-PTT-06 | Pain monitoring | An Achilles pain-monitoring model allowed continued activity without worse outcomes, but its numeric cutoffs are not validated for PTT. | RCT; 38 Achilles tendinopathy patients | No demonstrated harm versus active rest under the study protocol. | Low for PTT | Supports tracking delayed response and trend; do not copy Achilles numbers into a PTT rule. | Different tendon, diagnosis, rehab program, and population. | S034 |
| E-PTT-07 | Isometrics | Isometric exercise is not consistently superior to isotonic loading for tendinopathy; immediate pain response varies. | Systematic review/meta-analysis in non-PTT tendons | No consistent superiority. | Moderate for included tendons; low for PTT | Isometrics may remain a tolerated option, not a mandatory analgesic test. | No direct PTT trials. | S035 |
| E-REC-01 | Sleep | A night or more without sleep impairs performance on average; the effect of one ordinary suboptimal night or 1-3 nights of partial restriction is less certain. | Athlete consensus and meta-analysis | Acute sleep-loss endurance effect about SMD -0.52 in one meta-analysis. | Moderate | One poor night with normal symptoms should not automatically cancel easy training. | Laboratory deprivation often exceeds real-world sleep loss. | S037; S038 |
| E-REC-02 | Subjective monitoring | Subjective stress/fatigue can be useful for daily adaptation and, in one small trial, outperformed predefined and HRV-guided prescriptions. | Small RCT; 36 male recreational runners | Large within-group changes; tiny groups and 5-week duration. | Low | Supports a brief symptom/fatigue check-in as a coequal signal. | Potential expectancy and measurement bias; not independently replicated at scale. | S041 |
| E-REC-03 | HRV guidance | HRV-guided training can reduce the number of demanding sessions and may modestly improve some outcomes, but superiority is small/inconsistent. | Meta-analysis and two small RCTs | Performance SMD about 0.20 and nonsignificant in meta-analysis; trial results mixed. | Low-moderate | Use individualized multi-day trend as context, never a single-night veto. | Study HRV protocols differ from Garmin overnight readiness/status. | S039; S040; S042 |
| E-REC-04 | Readiness decisions | Readiness constructs and predictive validity are inconsistent; combining subjective and objective signals is more defensible than one score. | 2026 systematic review | No stable universal algorithm. | Low-moderate | Directly supports transparent multi-signal reasoning and uncertainty. | Field remains heterogeneous and fast-changing. | S043 |
| E-WEAR-01 | Garmin VO2max/race prediction | The Forerunner 265 can track general fitness trends, but individual VO2max and race predictions showed systematic error. | Device validation; 20 half-marathon trainees | VO2max overestimated ~2.6-2.9 mL/kg/min; race prediction ~7.6 min faster; MAPE <10%. | Low-moderate | Use direction over repeated standardized observations, not exact capability or race forecast. | Small sample; 12 weeks; one race distance. | S044 |
| E-WEAR-02 | Sleep metrics | Consumer wearables estimate sleep timing/duration better than sleep stages; device and firmware matter. | Lab validation; 53 adults | Garmin sleep/wake agreement 89%, kappa .35; stage agreement 50%, kappa .25. | Moderate for tested device | Use sleep duration/trend cautiously; de-emphasize stage minutes and composite score precision. | Forerunner 245, not 265; one lab night. | S045 |
| E-WEAR-03 | HRV metrics | Nocturnal wearable HRV can show within-device trends but agreement with ECG varies and is not interchangeable across devices. | 536-night validation; 13 adults | Garmin Fenix 6 CCC .87, MAPE 10.5% with wide individual error. | Low-moderate | Maintain same device/conditions and individual baseline; do not compare absolute HRV across platforms. | Different Garmin model; small sample. | S046 |
| E-WEAR-04 | Wrist heart rate | Wrist optical HR can be reasonable in steady treadmill running but errors and latency vary with condition and motion. | Validation; 29 adults | Running correlations .845-.906 in tested speeds; latency noted. | Low-moderate | Flag implausible traces and prefer chest strap for interval precision if desired. | Older Forerunner 235; correlation is not agreement. | S047 |
| E-WEAR-05 | Proprietary readiness | Garmin Training Readiness combines sleep score, recovery time, HRV status, acute load, sleep history, and stress according to the manufacturer; independent coaching-benefit validation is lacking. | Manufacturer documentation plus readiness review | No validated decision threshold. | High for metric description; insufficient for benefit | Expose components and conflicts; never let score override symptoms. | Proprietary algorithm and firmware changes. | S043; S048 |
| E-DATA-01 | Workload monitoring | Session RPE is practical and valid enough for broad load monitoring but is affected by heat, terrain, fatigue, psychology, and session type. | Review of 36 validation/reliability studies | Generally good validity/reliability; no universal conversion. | Moderate | A one-tap RPE can fill gaps and contextualize pace/HR. | Not injury prediction; retrospective bias possible. | S049 |
| E-DATA-02 | Pace-HR and decoupling | Pace/HR trends and decoupling can describe durability under comparable conditions, but no universal cutoff predicts readiness, injury, or race performance. | Large observational marathon analysis plus environmental study | Associations with performance; heat changes HR/pace relationship. | Low-moderate | Only compare similar route, weather, surface, device, effort, fueling, and fatigue. | Observational; metric definitions vary. | S050; S051 |
| E-FUEL-01 | Energy availability | Problematic low energy availability can impair health and performance, but REDs is a clinical, multifactorial syndrome and cannot be diagnosed from Garmin calories or fatigue alone. | IOC consensus and systematic review | Associations with performance, illness absence, and bone health; heterogeneous prevalence estimates. | Moderate for syndrome; low for individual inference | Persistent fatigue plus nutrition concerns should prompt discussion, not an automated diagnosis. | No single validated dashboard threshold; sex-specific and individual factors matter. | S052; S055 |
| E-FUEL-02 | Carbohydrate and recovery | Carbohydrate availability should scale with training demand; quality and longer sessions are more likely to benefit from deliberate fueling than short easy runs. | Professional position statement | Context-dependent recommendations; no single dose selected for this athlete. | Moderate | Use simple preparedness questions and optional planning, not mandatory calorie or gram tracking. | Exact needs depend on duration, intensity, goals, GI tolerance, body size, and total diet. | S053 |
| E-FUEL-03 | Protein and hybrid training | Adequate protein supports resistance-training adaptation; supplementation adds small average benefits when total intake is otherwise lower. | Meta-analysis; 49 trials, 1,863 adults | Strength +2.49 kg and fat-free mass +0.30 kg on average; plateau estimate ~1.62 g/kg/day in studied context. | Moderate for resistance training | Supports regular protein-containing meals; exact tracking remains optional. | Not a direct run-lift concurrent trial; supplement evidence is not a personalized target. | S054 |
| E-PER-01 | Block periodization | Block periodization may modestly improve VO2max/maximal power versus traditional training in trained athletes, but evidence is small and low quality. | Systematic review; 20 records, six pooled | VO2max SMD 0.40 [0.02, 0.79]; Wmax 0.28 [0.01, 0.54]. | Low | Possible later tool; poor fit for current low-volume tendon-limited rebuild. | Small samples, mean PEDro 3.7/10, trained populations. | S056 |

## Travel and time-zone addendum

**Search date:** 2026-11-22 (targeted review of air travel and time-zone change; it does not extend the package's comprehensive search date)
**Scope:** Rows E-TRAV-01–34 cite S073–S106. The population column marks directness: (a) direct evidence in endurance athletes; (b) other athletes or the general population, generalized; (c) expert consensus or coaching convention. Certainty is a pragmatic rating, not a GRADE assessment. Applicability states the coaching implication for a recreational runner who strength trains; `topics/travel_and_time_zones.md` applies these rows.

| Evidence ID | Question | Finding | Study population/design | Effect/uncertainty | Certainty | Applicability | Limitations | Source IDs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E-TRAV-01 | Scope and definitions | Travel fatigue can occur with any journey; jet lag specifically reflects circadian misalignment after rapid transmeridian travel, usually becoming more relevant around three or more zones. | (c) Consensus grounded in circadian physiology and athlete literature. | Travel duration, sleep loss, stress, and immobility can cause fatigue without a clock shift; zone change adds circadian desynchrony. | Moderate | The coach should model travel burden and circadian burden as separate variables. | The three-zone boundary is practical, not a biological cliff. | S073; S074; S075 |
| E-TRAV-02 | Direction | Eastward travel generally causes greater disruption than comparable westward travel because advancing the clock is usually harder than delaying it. | (b) Small athlete field studies and observational football cohorts; (c) circadian consensus. | Eight-zone crossover: eastward travel produced worse sleep, fatigue, motivation, day-one intermittent endurance (between-direction d about 1.06), and day-two sprint than westward. Larger cohorts show poorer eastward sleep, but effect sizes/explanatory power are small. | Moderate | Assign a higher initial adjustment burden to eastward trips, especially six or more zones, while retaining individual overrides. | Not universal; beyond about eight eastward zones, adaptation may occur by delaying rather than advancing. Studies are mostly team-sport athletes. | S073; S078; S104; S106 |
| E-TRAV-03 | Number of zones | More zones are associated with worse perceived fatigue and sleep, but zones explain only a minority of individual variation. | (b) Longitudinal elite-football analyses. | Across 355 trips, each zone was associated with poorer fatigue (β=.068) and sleep (β=.095); models explained 7–18% of variance. Nine or more zones were worse than fewer than six on days one and two. | Low-moderate | Use zones as a prior-risk input, not as the sole scheduler. | Observational data; route, schedule, athlete, and match context confound associations. | S104; S106 |
| E-TRAV-04 | “One day per zone” heuristic | Approximately one day per zone eastward and half a day per zone westward is an estimated circadian phase-shift rate, not a validated recovery or performance rule. | (b) General circadian physiology; (c) athlete consensus/model. | Consensus models assume roughly one-hour/day advances and two-hour/day delays. Symptom, sleep, and performance recovery do not move in lockstep and vary widely. | Low-moderate | The coach may use the heuristic only to estimate possible circadian misalignment and to plan light timing; it must not automatically block training for that many days. | Few athlete studies measure melatonin or core-temperature phase while also measuring performance. | S073; S075; S080; S081 |
| E-TRAV-05 | Sleep and perceived recovery | Sleep and subjective fatigue are more consistently disrupted than measured performance. | (a) Small triathlete/cyclist cohorts; (b) systematic reviews and team-sport cohorts. | Triathletes lost 4.8±1.2 h in-flight sleep and had a moderate fatigue rise; cyclists lost 268 min during travel and 197 min on arrival night one. Reviews found performance decrements in only a minority of studies. | Moderate | Give actual sleep and symptoms substantial weight even when planned pace/power looks achievable; do not infer performance failure from symptoms alone. | Actigraphy and self-report; small cohorts; no running-race outcomes. | S077; S079; S080; S103 |
| E-TRAV-06 | Endurance performance | Insufficient evidence establishes a predictable decrement in continuous endurance running after transmeridian flight. | (a) Very limited endurance-athlete evidence; (b) soldiers and intermittent team-sport tests generalized. | The 2025 review found only four endurance-related studies, all using intermittent Yo-Yo-type tests rather than constant-power endurance. An older soldier study found slower 2.8-km running on days two to three after six-zone eastward travel. | Low | Avoid numeric pace penalties. Use an easy first exposure and athlete-specific response before prescribing high-stakes pace work. | No direct recreational-distance-running or concurrent-training trial; repeated-test and environmental confounding. | S075; S078; S082 |
| E-TRAV-07 | Perceived exertion | Exercise RPE after travel is inconsistent. | (b) Simulated long travel and healthy-male field evidence. | A 24-hour travel simulation worsened effort/mood with reduced intermittent performance; the six-zone soldier study found no treadmill RPE change despite slower field running. | Low | Treat elevated RPE as a same-day readiness signal, not an inevitable jet-lag effect or proof of lost fitness. | Different protocols, old evidence, nonrunner populations, and no standardized timing by body clock. | S082; S084 |
| E-TRAV-08 | Recovery duration | Different outcomes recover on different timelines: approximately 48 hours in some endurance cohorts, three to four days for selected performance measures, and five to seven or more days for some symptoms/sleep measures. | (a) Small endurance cohorts; (b) athlete field studies. | Triathlete/cyclist sleep disruption concentrated in first 48 h. Team-sport performance effects often peaked in first 72 h. Lemmer reported symptoms to days five to seven; one soccer cohort reported symptoms to day 13 despite preserved readiness. | Low-moderate | Use rolling reassessment rather than one recovery countdown. Sleep, symptoms, easy-session response, and key-session importance should determine progression. | Measures and populations differ; “near baseline” was defined differently; environmental and competition effects persist. | S078; S079; S080; S081; S088; S103 |
| E-TRAV-09 | Strength and neuromuscular performance | Sleep-disruptive long-haul travel may transiently impair jump/reactive performance, but direct maximal-strength evidence is insufficient. | (b) Rugby/team-sport cohorts and reviews; generalized to strength training. | Overnight long-haul travel reduced reactive jump about 7% on arrival day one and it remained below home values on day three in one study. Reviews find mixed strength/power responses and no robust 1RM evidence. | Low | Avoid automatically scheduling maximal, high-skill, or high-volume lifting immediately after an overnight/major-zone trip; gate it by sleep and warm-up response. | Jump performance is not equivalent to maximal strength; no recreational concurrent runners. | S075; S077; S088 |
| E-TRAV-10 | Acute sleep loss as a mediator | Lost sleep can reduce physical performance independently of jet lag. | (b) Meta-analysis of general exercise populations. | Across 227 outcomes, acute sleep loss averaged −7.56% performance (95% CI −11.9 to −3.13), with I²=98.1%; late-night/partial restriction and afternoon testing were especially vulnerable. | Moderate | Overnight flights, red-eyes, and very early departures should trigger a sleep-loss adjustment even with zero zones crossed. | Huge heterogeneity; 89% male; not a travel-specific dose and not a prediction for an individual. | S096 |
| E-TRAV-11 | Early departures and red-eyes | An itinerary that truncates biological-night sleep can impair next-day training even when the flight itself is short. | (b) Randomized simulated short-haul study plus general sleep-loss evidence. | Two- and four-hour early-morning simulations preceded by reduced sleep produced next-day decrements of about 3.5% repeated-sprint, 6% 10-m sprint, and 5.5% jump/football-test performance. | Low-moderate | Model sleep opportunity and wake time explicitly. A short red-eye is not a “minor trip.” | Simulation; highly trained male footballers; cannot isolate early wake, sitting, and testing effects. | S085; S096 |
| E-TRAV-12 | Short daytime flight, no zone change | A short daytime flight with preserved sleep usually needs little or no training adjustment. | (b) Small domestic/simulated team-sport studies. | Five-hour simulated domestic travel showed little effect; short-haul rugby travel increased sleepiness/leg tiredness without clear strength/power loss; soccer findings were equivocal. | Low-moderate | Default to the planned session when sleep, symptoms, and logistics are normal; keep an easy fallback rather than preemptively deleting training. | Small samples, team-sport outcomes, and travel logistics vary. | S084; S086; S087 |
| E-TRAV-13 | Long travel without zone change | Long duration can reduce training quality through sleep loss and confinement even without circadian shift. | (b) Simulated 24-hour travel and sleep-loss evidence; (c) consensus. | Twenty-four-hour simulated travel reduced next-afternoon intermittent performance and sleep. Travel fatigue is conceptually independent of time-zone change. | Low-moderate | Use door-to-door duration and sleep overlap, not just zone count, to decide whether the next session should be easy or deferred. | Simulation and team-sport testing; no continuous running trial. | S073; S084; S096 |
| E-TRAV-14 | Cabin humidity and dehydration | Low humidity can cause dryness, but meaningful flight-induced whole-body dehydration and its performance effect are unproven. | (b) Narrative review of athlete and general-flight studies. | Mechanisms and fluid shifts are plausible; the review found no clear evidence for incidence, magnitude, duration, or performance impact in athletes. | Low | Encourage normal access to fluids and routine hydration habits, but do not prescribe forced drinking or label poor training as dehydration without evidence. | No standardized hydration measures across travel studies; food, caffeine, alcohol, and individual intake confound results. | S099 |
| E-TRAV-15 | Cabin hypoxia | Commercial cabin pressure can lower oxygen saturation modestly, but evidence that this causes next-day endurance loss is insufficient. | (b) Athlete flight observation and hypobaric simulation. | National athletes showed an in-flight SpO₂ decline of about 3–4% with recovery after landing. Recovery-sleep changes in simulation were clearer at 12,000 ft than at 8,000 ft, the latter closer to commercial cabins. | Low | Do not treat an ordinary flight as altitude training or assume a performance decrement from transient SpO₂ change alone. | No postflight endurance endpoint; cabin pressure, illness, and individual susceptibility vary. | S097; S098 |
| E-TRAV-16 | Prolonged sitting | Sitting contributes to stiffness, leg discomfort, fluid pooling, and VTE risk; its independent effect on next-day running quality is insufficiently quantified. | (b) Flight physiology/general population; (c) public-health guidance. | Domestic athlete studies report leg tiredness; VTE guidance identifies immobility as a central risk pathway. No clean trial isolates sitting from sleep and schedule. | Low-moderate | Allow movement opportunities and expect possible stiffness; use warm-up response to modify training rather than assuming injury or performance loss. | No athlete dose-response for sitting duration and training performance. | S086; S099; S100 |
| E-TRAV-17 | Training before versus after flight | Insufficient evidence directly compares a hard session before versus after a flight. | (c) Consensus and coaching convention; no direct comparative athlete trial found. | Consensus emphasizes pretravel sleep protection, avoiding accumulated load, and low-to-moderate activity after arrival; no tested superiority of “before” or “after.” | Low | If a quality session must occur on travel day, prefer the option that preserves sleep, recovery time, and safe logistics; after overnight/long-haul arrival, default to easy/rest unless readiness is clearly normal. | Convention may be confounded by team schedules and practical constraints. | S073; S074 |
| E-TRAV-18 | Easy versus hard after arrival | Low-to-moderate activity after arrival is a reasonable default; evidence that it accelerates adaptation or recovery is insufficient. | (c) Athlete consensus; (b) laboratory exercise phase-response evidence. | Exercise can shift circadian phase depending on clock time, but no travel trial shows that an easy run reliably improves athlete adaptation or subsequent performance. | Low | Use easy activity for routine, circulation, and a readiness check—not as a prescribed cure for jet lag. Avoid hard work when sleepiness or coordination is impaired. | Mistimed exercise may shift the clock in the wrong direction; phase-response study was laboratory-based. | S073; S093 |
| E-TRAV-19 | Timing relative to departure/arrival | Protecting sleep opportunity is better supported than a universal exercise clock-time rule. | (b) Sleep-loss meta-analysis and schedule studies; (c) consensus. | Early/late arrivals and morning departures are associated with poorer responses in some cohorts. Arrival during daytime was better than early morning/late night in one football dataset; 2026 female data found morning arrivals better than evening, illustrating context dependence. | Low-moderate | Choose training times that do not sacrifice sleep; record local arrival time and biological-night overlap. Use destination-time exercise cautiously. | Observational results conflict by route/context; travel variables explain little variance. | S096; S105; S106 |
| E-TRAV-20 | Short trips and home time | Keeping home-time sleep can reduce layover symptoms for a very short trip, but evidence is limited to one westward study. | (b) Crossover field study in 19 flight attendants; (c) AASM option. | During a 50-hour, nine-zone westward layover, home-time sleep reduced sleepiness/jet-lag symptoms; it did not improve post-return outcomes, prolonged wake before first home sleep, and one-third preferred local time. | Low | For a stay of about two days or less, home-time or partial home-time may be considered only when event/work times permit. | E-TRAV-24 | Returning home | Return travel is another exposure; readaptation time is variable and a fixed delay for quality sessions or long runs is unsupported. | (b) Athlete field cohorts; (c) inference. | Eastward return was worse than westward outbound in one crossover. The home-time layover strategy did not improve post-return symptoms. | Low-moderate | Recompute burden for the return itinerary and gate the next quality/long session by actual sleep and an easy-session or warm-up check. | E-TRAV-25 | Race travel | No trial establishes an optimal arrival day for an endurance race across time zones. | (b) Athlete field studies; (c) inference/convention. | First 48–72 h are commonly the most disrupted. | E-TRAV-28 | Unfamiliar terrain and hotel treadmills | Insufficient evidence supports a travel-specific rule for unfamiliar terrain or hotel treadmills. | (a, b) General treadmill-versus-overground evidence; (c) coaching convention. | Physiology and RPE vary with treadmill speed/grade; treadmill endurance performance was lower on average (SMD −0.50±0.36). | E-TRAV-29 | VTE risk boundary | Long-distance travel slightly increases VTE risk, but absolute risk is low in healthy travelers and most cases involve additional risk factors. | (b) General-population epidemiology synthesized by CDC; (c) public-health guidance. | For flights over four hours, symptomatic VTE estimates are about one event per 4,656–6,000 person-flights; relative-risk estimates are roughly two- to fourfold but inconsistent.

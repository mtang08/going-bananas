# Evidence Matrix Addendum: Periodization, Calibration, Workouts, and Transitions

**Search date:** 2026-11-11  
**Scope:** Focused incremental review for package `1.1.0+privacy1`. This file extends, and does not replace, `references/evidence_matrix.md`. Existing evidence IDs remain authoritative for aerobic progression, intensity distribution, long runs, concurrent strength, symptoms, recovery, wearables, and fueling.

Certainty describes confidence in the research finding. Applicability separately addresses transfer to low-to-moderate-volume recreational running with approximately 3–4 runs per week. A software horizon or coaching convention is not promoted to a physiological fact.

| Evidence ID | Question | Finding | Study population/design | Effect/uncertainty | Certainty | Applicability | Limitations | Source IDs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E-PER-02 | How strongly is classical periodization doctrine validated? | Periodization supplies useful planning language, but many fixed sequencing assumptions and proposed biological mechanisms have limited direct empirical validation. A structured, revisable plan is more defensible than treating a prewritten sequence as physiological law. | Critical narrative reviews of periodization theory and evidence | No effect estimate; conceptual and evidence-quality critique. | Moderate for the critique; low for selecting one superior model | High for preventing rigid product logic; does not identify the best block length or model. | Narrative rather than systematic outcome comparison; critique does not imply that planning is useless. | S057; S058 |
| E-PER-03 | How do successful distance runners commonly sequence training? | Descriptive elite literature commonly reports broad general-to-specific sequencing, high easy-volume foundations, hard/easy organization, and increasing event-specific work near competition. | Systematic review and integrative review of highly trained/world-class distance runners | Consistent broad pattern, but no causal estimate for any single sequencing feature. | Moderate for description; low for causal superiority | Useful for broad phase logic and maintenance of foundational work; poor basis for copying elite volume, density, or exact workouts. | Elite, predominantly male, high-volume contexts; practice data and observational reports; publication and survivorship bias. | S059; S060 |
| E-PER-04 | What does experienced-coach practice add? | World-class coaches report pragmatic, individualized planning, monitoring, and adjustment rather than one invariant model. Their practice supports responsive review, but does not causally validate the methods or transfer the doses. | Mixed-methods study of Norwegian world-class endurance coaches | Descriptive convergence; no randomized comparison. | Low-moderate | Supports labeling flexible review and individualized modification as coaching practice. | Elite support systems and selection; self-report; national coaching culture; no recreational outcome comparison. | S061 |
| E-PER-05 | Is a fixed 4–8-week explicit block superior for a recreational runner on 3–4 runs/week? | No direct validating trial was located. Limited block-periodization evidence in trained athletes suggests possible small gains in selected outcomes, while broader periodization literature does not establish a universal duration or superiority. Explicit blocks should therefore be optional coordination tools with planned reviews and completion/early-review conditions. | Prior small systematic review plus targeted periodization and endurance-practice literature | Prior pooled estimates were small and based on low-quality studies; no direct low-volume recreational comparison. | Low | High for rejecting a hard-coded block duration or mandatory block entity; low for choosing an exact duration. | Population mismatch, small samples, heterogeneous definitions, and indirect inference. | S056; S057; S058; S059; S060; S061 |
| E-CAL-01 | Can field critical-speed protocols establish intensity anchors? | Several field protocols can estimate critical speed reliably when tests, mathematical model, environment, and execution are standardized. Estimates—especially D-prime—remain protocol- and model-sensitive. | Systematic review of runner field-test protocols | Many protocols reported high reliability; between-protocol/model agreement is not guaranteed. | Moderate | Useful when an athlete accepts repeatable testing and the app stores the exact protocol; unnecessary for routine easy training. | Study and protocol heterogeneity; many small samples; reliability is not identical to physiological validity or interchangeability. | S064 |
| E-CAL-02 | Are noninvasive threshold methods interchangeable? | Talk test, ventilatory surrogates, HR-derived methods, critical speed, and other field estimates may correlate at group level but can disagree for an individual. Agreement and repeatability under a named protocol matter more than correlation alone. | Evidence-based narrative review of 88 reports | Direction and accuracy vary by method, criterion, population, and protocol; no universal conversion. | Moderate | High for requiring method/date/confidence metadata and effort-first fallbacks. | Narrative synthesis; heterogeneous threshold definitions and criterion measures; not every method has equivalent field feasibility. | S065 |
| E-CAL-03 | How should critical speed and D-prime be used in running? | Critical speed can help model severe-domain performance and structure selected sessions, but protocol, mathematical model, trial duration, and day-to-day execution materially affect the estimate; D-prime is generally less stable. | Scoping review of critical-speed/D-prime measurement and application | Broad support for utility with substantial methodological variation; no single best protocol for every setting. | Moderate | Optional higher-detail anchor for a motivated athlete; not a required dashboard truth or one-number zone system. | Scoping review does not pool one effect; direct low-volume recreational prescription evidence is limited. | S066 |
| E-CAL-04 | Can one test or block establish individual response or nonresponse? | A single pre/post comparison can misclassify response because biological and measurement variability are substantial. Repeated comparable observations and exposure/adherence context are needed before declaring a stable individual response pattern. | Repeated-testing training study plus systematic review of individual-response methods | Responder classifications vary by analytical method; many studies inadequately separate true response heterogeneity from noise. | Moderate-high for the measurement warning | High for preventing one-race recalibration, one-block “nonresponder” labels, or wholesale plan changes from a single noisy result. | General exercise populations and methods literature; does not specify the exact number of repetitions needed for every running measure. | S067; S068 |
| E-WKT-01 | Does research define one optimal interval recipe? | Interval response depends on interacting work duration, recovery, intensity, repetition count, modality, and accumulated mechanical/neuromuscular strain. Time near VO2max is one possible target, not a complete prescription or proof of superiority. | Major interval-programming reviews | Multiple configurations can create a stimulus; no universal winner or safe recreational dose. | Moderate for multivariable programming; low for exact starter doses | High for recipe design that separates intent, prerequisites, progression levers, and mechanical cost. | Reviews include trained/elite examples; direct injury and low-frequency recreational comparisons are sparse. | S069; S070; S071 |
| E-RACE-01 | What general taper strategy is supported? | Endurance performance generally improves when accumulated load is reduced before competition while familiar intensity and, often, frequency are retained. Meta-analytic subgroup findings favor substantial volume reduction and tapers no longer than roughly three weeks, but heterogeneity prevents universal percentages or durations. | Systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 endurance-taper studies | Reported improvements in time-trial and time-to-exhaustion outcomes; subgroup estimates varied by taper structure. | Moderate | Supports the principle “reduce fatigue while retaining familiar readiness,” not a fixed taper recipe. | Mixed sports, populations, outcome types, and taper definitions; subgroup analyses can overstate precision. | S062 |
| E-RACE-02 | What does recreational-marathon evidence add? | In a large observational dataset, more disciplined and longer tapers were associated with better marathon performance than minimal tapering. The association supports planning a taper, but does not identify the causal or optimal individual reduction. | Observational app-data study of more than 158,000 recreational marathon runners | Longer disciplined tapers were associated with improved performance; effect depends on the comparison and athlete subgroup. | Moderate for association; low for causal prescription | Relevant future-facing evidence for recreational marathon planning. | Self-selection, confounding by fitness/coaching/adherence, imperfect activity capture, and marathon-specific context. | S063 |
| E-TRANS-01 | What happens after training interruption? | Complete or substantial endurance-training cessation can reduce cardiorespiratory and metabolic adaptations within weeks, while some retained stimulus can mitigate losses. Magnitude depends on prior training, interruption length, and what activity continues. | Systematic review of detraining in endurance athletes | Direction is consistent; exact timing and magnitude vary widely. | Moderate | Supports staged restoration and block reassessment instead of compressed make-up training. | Trained-athlete focus, heterogeneous interruption definitions, and no universal day-by-day loss or return formula. | S072 |

## Integration rules

1. Load this addendum for `review_training_block`, `recalibrate_intensity`, `handle_goal_or_race_change`, recipe audits, or consequential disputes. Routine daily checks should not retrieve it.
2. `E-PER-05` records an evidence gap. It is not evidence that explicit blocks are ineffective; it prevents a software default from masquerading as validated physiology.
3. Taper subgroup values are examples from a heterogeneous literature, not executable percentages or durations.
4. Critical speed, threshold, and race-derived anchors are stored with method, date, protocol/conditions, quality, and confidence. They are not silently converted into one interchangeable zone system.
5. Every candidate action remains subordinate to current symptoms/function, clinician instructions, user priorities, and the approved plan version.

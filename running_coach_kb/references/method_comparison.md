# Training Method Comparison

**Purpose.** This file compares candidate methods for a hybrid recreational runner rebuilding low-to-moderate running volume while managing posterior-tibial-region symptoms. It is a routing aid, not a menu that should be stacked together. “Studied dose” records what research tested; it is **not** an automatic personal prescription.

**Evidence labels used here**

- **Research:** finding directly supported by the cited study or review.
- **Clinical guidance:** professional or clinical recommendation, not necessarily trial-tested as a coaching rule.
- **Coaching convention:** common practice without direct validating evidence.
- **Inference:** a transparent application to this athlete from indirect evidence.

## 1. Predominantly easy / pyramidal training

**Purpose.** Build aerobic volume while retaining a smaller amount of moderate and high-intensity work. In a three-zone model, pyramidal training usually means most time below the first threshold, less between thresholds, and least above the second threshold.

**Studied populations and doses.** Endurance studies pool runners, cyclists, skiers, rowers, and triathletes across recreational to elite levels. Intervention periods are commonly several weeks to a few months, with intensity classified by varying methods. The individual-participant network meta-analysis found no overall polarized-versus-pyramidal winner, and recreational athletes may respond at least as well to pyramidal training [E-INT-02; S010].

**Role now.** **Inference:** This is the best descriptive default during a tendon-limited rebuild: most running truly easy, one controlled quality exposure when tolerated, and a long run that is not automatically treated as “easy” merely because pace is low. Do not enforce a fixed percentage.

**Prerequisites and monitoring.** Reliable easy-effort cues, session RPE, symptom tracking, and a distinction between time-in-zone and session goal. Monitor whether the long run or lifting creates a second/third hard exposure.

**Costs and interactions.** Low-intensity running still creates repetitive tendon load; a high share of easy work is not a guarantee of low mechanical stress. Meaningful lifting remains in the plan.

**Alternatives.** Temporarily all-easy running during illness return or symptom flare; low-impact aerobic substitution.

**Confidence.** Moderate that this is effective; low that a precise distribution is optimal.

**Sources.** E-INT-01–04; S009–S013.

## 2. Polarized training

**Purpose.** Combine a large volume below the first threshold with a small amount above the second threshold and little work between them.

**Studied populations and doses.** Small trials and meta-analyses use incompatible zone systems and often include trained athletes at greater volume. One recreational-runner trial found both polarized and between-threshold programs improved 10 km performance, without a statistically significant primary between-group difference [E-INT-01; S009]. A 2024 meta-analysis found a small VO2peak advantage but no clear advantage for several performance outcomes [E-INT-03; S011].

**Role now.** **Inference:** It may describe a plan with easy runs plus a clear quality session, but it should not generate an 80/20 target. With the agreed rolling-cycle run count, one session changes session-count percentages dramatically; time-in-zone can also be distorted by warm-up and recovery.

**Prerequisites.** Stable volume, a robust tendon, enough easy volume to support a genuinely small hard fraction, and clear intensity anchors.

**Costs/interactions.** The “hard” component may be mechanically expensive, and lifting adds another high-neuromuscular exposure.

**Alternatives.** Pyramidal or predominantly easy training; threshold-oriented singles in a later phase.

**Confidence.** Moderate that it can work; low that it is categorically superior or that a ratio transfers to this athlete.

**Sources.** E-INT-01–04; S009–S013.

## 3. Threshold-oriented single sessions

**Purpose.** Accumulate controlled work near a sustainable heavy-intensity boundary to improve speed at threshold and race-relevant aerobic capacity.

**Studied populations and doses.** Protocols vary from continuous tempo runs to repetitions of several minutes with short recoveries. “Threshold” may mean lactate, ventilatory, critical-speed, pace, or RPE anchors; these are not interchangeable. The recreational polarized-versus-between-threshold trial shows that threshold-oriented work can improve performance but not that it is universally best [E-INT-01; S009].

**Role now.** **Inference:** A controlled interval form can be one quality-session option once easy running and delayed PTT response are stable. Intervals may make intensity easier to control than a long continuous tempo. It should not become an additional weekly session during the rebuild.

**Prerequisites.** Reproducible effort anchor, conservative total work, intact warm-up mechanics, no worsening delayed response, and adequate carbohydrate availability for the session.

**Costs/interactions.** Moderate physiological fatigue and more fast-running mechanical load than easy running. Avoid pairing with unfamiliar high-soreness lifting.

**Monitoring.** RPE progression, ability to hold form, pace/HR only under comparable conditions, tendon response through the next day, and subsequent strength quality.

**Alternatives.** Fartlek, short aerobic intervals, easy running plus relaxed strides, or no quality during an unstable period.

**Confidence.** Low–moderate for choosing among formats; moderate that controlled threshold work can improve aerobic performance.

**Sources.** E-INT-01, E-INT-04, E-INT-07; S009, S012, S013, S017–S019.

## 4. Norwegian single/double-threshold approaches

**Purpose.** Accumulate a large amount of controlled threshold work, often guided by blood lactate, sometimes split into morning and evening sessions to reduce acute strain per session.

**Studied populations and doses.** Descriptive work concerns elite Norwegian endurance athletes. An acute crossover in 14 elite men split 60 minutes of threshold work into two sessions and observed lower acute drift/fatigue markers than one session; it did not test long-term performance superiority [E-INT-06; S015–S016].

**Role now.** None as a default. **Inference:** The athlete’s low running frequency, concurrent lifting, uncertain thresholds, and PTT constraint eliminate the conditions that make elite double-threshold practice interpretable.

**Prerequisites for any later consideration.** Much higher stable volume, measured/validated intensity control, strong fueling and recovery capacity, robust tissue tolerance, and a reason that simpler training no longer suffices.

**Costs/interactions.** Large total workload, two daily exposures, glycogen demand, and possible interference with lifting. Lactate targets from elite systems must not be imported.

**Alternatives.** One controlled threshold session; pyramidal training; progression through easy volume first.

**Confidence.** Low direct applicability; evidence supports description and acute response, not superiority.

**Sources.** E-INT-06; S015–S016.

## 5. “Zone 2” prescriptions

**Purpose.** Usually denotes sustainable low-intensity aerobic work, but common five-zone systems may call different physiological domains “Zone 2.”

**Studied populations and doses.** An expert viewpoint favored intensity immediately below the first lactate/ventilatory threshold and emphasized that desired adaptations are not exclusive to a narrow zone [E-INT-05; S014].

**Role now.** Use plain-language anchors—conversational, controlled, low RPE—and retain the source of each zone definition. A Garmin Zone 2 label should never be assumed equivalent to a laboratory first-threshold prescription.

**Prerequisites.** Correct max-HR/threshold settings if HR zones are used; good sensor signal; awareness of heat, hills, and cardiac drift.

**Costs/interactions.** Chasing an HR ceiling can create unnatural slowing or excessive duration; conversely, bad zones can turn easy runs moderate.

**Alternatives.** Talk test, RPE, pace under standardized conditions, or laboratory/field threshold assessment.

**Confidence.** Low–moderate for terminology guidance; no evidence for a universally optimal narrow band.

**Sources.** E-INT-04–05, E-WEAR-04; S012–S014, S047.

## 6. Multi-minute VO2 intervals

**Purpose.** Accumulate time near high oxygen uptake and improve high-end aerobic power.

**Studied populations and doses.** Trials include structures such as 4 × 4 minutes, often two to three times weekly for several weeks—far more hard work than is appropriate to import here. One trial reported a 6.5% VO2max improvement in its tested high-dose program, while network evidence does not establish a universal interval winner [E-INT-07; S017–S019].

**Role now.** Not a rebuild priority. **Inference:** Introduce later as a small, phase-specific dose when mileage, PTT response, and lifting coexist reliably. Mile/5K goals can be served by periodic high-end work without making it year-round.

**Prerequisites.** Stable easy/long running, reliable warm-up, adequate recovery/fueling, and no symptom response to faster running.

**Costs/interactions.** High cardiometabolic fatigue, faster mechanics, and possible calf/foot stress. Schedule away from high-soreness lower-body strength or consolidate deliberately after calibration.

**Monitoring.** Rep consistency, RPE, technique, next-day symptoms, and effect on later lifting/long run—not merely time in a watch zone.

**Alternatives.** Shorter aerobic intervals, controlled hills, threshold work, or easy volume.

**Confidence.** Low–moderate for format selection; good general evidence that intervals can improve fitness.

**Sources.** E-INT-07; S017–S019.

## 7. Short aerobic intervals and 10–20–30-style work

**Purpose.** Accumulate changes of pace with short bouts and recoveries, potentially reducing perceived burden while retaining a cardiovascular stimulus.

**Studied populations and doses.** Small trials in recreational/well-trained runners use varying short-interval formats and often multiple weekly sessions [E-INT-07; S017–S019]. The label does not guarantee low mechanical load: the fastest segments may be very fast.

**Role now.** Possible later quality format if controlled below sprinting and if it proves easier to recover from than long repetitions. Do not infer that “short” means safe for PTT.

**Prerequisites.** Precise instructions, a cap on effort, flat predictable surface, and stable symptom response.

**Costs/interactions.** Repeated accelerations can increase mechanical demand; pace may be difficult to measure accurately with wrist GPS over very short bouts.

**Monitoring.** RPE, form, acceleration behavior, next-day symptoms, and overall work; avoid overinterpreting instantaneous pace.

**Alternatives.** Fartlek by feel, threshold repetitions, strides, or steady easy running.

**Confidence.** Low–moderate.

**Sources.** E-INT-07, E-WEAR-04; S017–S019, S047.

## 8. Relaxed strides

**Purpose.** Brief, non-exhaustive exposures to faster coordinated running, often used to retain speed familiarity.

**Studied populations and doses.** Direct trials establishing a minimum effective stride dose, injury effect, or PTT-safe progression in recreational runners were not located [E-INT-08]. Do not equate strides with all-out sprint intervals.

**Role now.** Optional only after stable easy-running response. **Inference:** They may help mile/5K compatibility while aerobic development remains primary, but the benefit-to-risk balance is athlete-specific.

**Prerequisites.** No worsening symptoms, smooth acceleration/deceleration, forgiving surface, full recovery, and no fatigue-driven straining.

**Costs/interactions.** Higher peak speed and plantarflexor demand; strength/plyometric load must be counted.

**Monitoring.** Quality of movement, not watch pace; immediate and delayed PTT response.

**Alternatives.** Technique drills, very short controlled pickups, or no speed exposure until tolerance improves.

**Confidence.** Insufficient direct evidence; transparent coaching inference.

**Sources.** E-INT-08; S017, S019.

## 9. Short hills and hill sprints

**Purpose.** Develop force, coordination, and speed with potentially lower absolute speed but high propulsive demand.

**Studied populations and doses.** There is no direct evidence here validating a PTT-safe dose or superior outcome for this athlete. “Hill sprint” can range from relaxed short uphill running to maximal sprinting; these are not equivalent.

**Role now.** Not a default while posterior-tibial-region load tolerance is uncertain. Later, controlled hills may be introduced as a separate mechanical progression—not casually added to a volume or intensity increase.

**Prerequisites.** Calf/soleus and foot loading tolerance, stable flat running, appropriate surface, and no clinician restriction.

**Costs/interactions.** High calf/soleus and foot demand, acceleration, and possible DOMS; can conflict with lower-body strength and long runs.

**Monitoring.** Symptom response during and a later follow-up window, mechanics, and recovery.

**Alternatives.** Flat strides, controlled incline walking/cycling, or strength work.

**Confidence.** Insufficient direct evidence for this case.

**Sources.** E-INT-08, E-PTT-02; S017, S019, S028–S030.

## 10. Long-run development

**Purpose.** Extend aerobic durability, fueling familiarity, and running-specific musculoskeletal tolerance.

**Studied populations and doses.** Observational studies associate weekly volume and longer endurance runs with half/marathon performance, but no universal safe long-run percentage is validated [E-LONG-01–02; S020–S021].

**Role now.** Continue a controlled long run only to the extent that delayed PTT response remains acceptable. The reported long run at an observed pace cannot be classified by pace alone. Progress duration rather than chasing pace, and do not increase long-run size simultaneously with new quality or plyometrics.

**Prerequisites.** Stable shorter runs, sufficient recovery/fueling, and a reliable delayed-symptom check.

**Costs/interactions.** High single-session mechanical load; at low weekly volume it may dominate the week even when “easy.”

**Monitoring.** Recent longest comparable run, duration, terrain, RPE, HR quality, symptoms during and after, and later response.

**Alternatives.** Repeat duration, split aerobic work across an easy run and cross-training, or use a shorter long run.

**Confidence.** Moderate for performance association; low for individual dose.

**Sources.** E-AER-03, E-LONG-01–03; S003, S020–S023.

## 11. Hard-day consolidation

**Purpose.** Place demanding run and strength work on the same day or adjacent period so other days remain genuinely easy.

**Studied populations and doses.** Concurrent-training evidence suggests maximal strength and hypertrophy are generally compatible, but explosive strength may be more vulnerable and same-session design matters [E-HYB-01; S024]. Direct trials comparing this athlete’s weekly arrangements are absent.

**Role now.** A scheduling option—not a command. Consolidation can protect easy days but may create an intolerable single-day load. The higher-priority session should generally come first, with separation when practical, then calibrated by actual soreness and quality.

**Prerequisites.** Known response to both sessions, control of lower-body strength volume/proximity to failure, and a backup plan.

**Costs/interactions.** Acute fatigue may reduce the second session; combined tendon loading may be high.

**Monitoring.** Second-session quality, next-day soreness, gait, PTT delayed response, and whether protected days are actually easy.

**Alternatives.** Separate days, easy-run-plus-lift pairing, or reduce lower-body lifting dose near key runs.

**Confidence.** Moderate for broad compatibility; low for exact placement.

**Sources.** E-HYB-01, E-HYB-04; S024.

## 12. Mixed/traditional periodization

**Purpose.** Maintain multiple qualities while shifting emphasis across phases rather than fully concentrating one ability.

**Studied populations and doses.** “Traditional” is heterogeneous and often serves as the comparator in periodization studies. Direct evidence for one ideal sequence in recreational hybrid athletes is insufficient.

**Role now.** **Inference/coaching convention:** Maintain easy aerobic work, one controlled quality exposure, one long run, and two strength sessions, while progressing only one major running dimension. This serves both long-term endurance and later mile/5K work without pretending all goals peak together.

**Prerequisites.** Clear phase priority and limits on the number of progressing stimuli.

**Costs/interactions.** Too many “maintenance” elements can become too many demanding exposures.

**Monitoring.** 14-day plan density, symptoms, quality of key sessions, and adherence.

**Alternatives.** Short all-easy consolidation; a later focused block.

**Confidence.** Coaching convention with indirect support.

**Sources.** E-AER-02, E-INT-02, E-HYB-01, E-PER-01; S002, S010, S024, S056.

## 13. Block periodization

**Purpose.** Concentrate a particular training stimulus for a defined block, then shift emphasis.

**Studied populations and doses.** A systematic review found small pooled advantages for VO2max and maximal power, but only six studies entered meta-analysis and methodological quality was low [E-PER-01; S056]. Participants were generally trained athletes, not low-volume tendon-limited hybrid athletes.

**Role now.** Poor fit. A rebuild already functions as a broad aerobic/tolerance phase; adding concentrated intensity would reduce observability and raise load.

**Possible later role.** A carefully bounded emphasis block after stable volume, when a specific race or plateau justifies it and maintenance of strength/other qualities is planned.

**Costs/interactions.** Concentrated fatigue, possible loss of neglected qualities, and conflict with lifting.

**Alternatives.** Mixed periodization with one changing emphasis.

**Confidence.** Low.

**Sources.** E-PER-01; S056.

## 14. Cross-training

**Purpose.** Add or preserve aerobic work while reducing running-specific impact/load.

**Studied populations and doses.** A small cycling-substitution trial maintained estimated VO2max over five weeks, and a 2026 meta-analysis of seven randomized trials found no clear between-group advantage, with wide uncertainty [E-LONG-03; S022–S023].

**Role now.** Useful when PTT tolerance limits running but systemic recovery allows aerobic work. It should not silently increase total fatigue or replace every run.

**Prerequisites.** A tolerated mode, mode-specific RPE calibration, and compatible strength recovery.

**Costs/interactions.** Cycling may add quadriceps fatigue; elliptical may still load the foot; pool work has skill/access constraints. Running economy and tissue durability do not transfer one-for-one.

**Monitoring.** Mode RPE, HR only within that mode, symptoms, and effect on lifting.

**Alternatives.** Rest, shorter running, or splitting aerobic time.

**Confidence.** Low–moderate.

**Sources.** E-LONG-03; S022–S023.

## 15. All-out sprint interval training

**Purpose.** Produce a high-intensity stimulus in very little total work.

**Studied populations and doses.** Sprint-interval protocols often use maximal or near-maximal bouts and are physiologically distinct from relaxed strides. They are not required to achieve this athlete’s aerobic-first goals, and the evidence reviewed does not establish a favorable PTT risk-benefit balance.

**Role now.** None. Later use would require a specific reason, robust tissue preparation, and separation from other explosive work.

**Prerequisites.** High mechanical readiness, sprint familiarity, long recovery, and an appropriate surface.

**Costs/interactions.** Very high acceleration, calf/foot demand, neuromuscular fatigue, and interference with lifting/quality running.

**Alternatives.** Multi-minute VO2 intervals, short controlled aerobic intervals, relaxed strides.

**Confidence.** Low applicability.

**Sources.** E-INT-07–08, E-HYB-02; S017–S019, S025.

## Decision rule for the future agent

Select the **least complex method that serves the current phase** and is supported by current tolerance. The normal sequence is: establish continuity → stabilize symptoms and easy/long running → progress one load dimension → retain a small calibrated speed/quality exposure → specialize only when a race or plateau justifies it. A method’s popularity, elite use, or watch label is not evidence that its dose belongs in this plan.

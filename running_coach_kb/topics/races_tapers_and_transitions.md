# Topic Guide: Races, Benchmarks, Tapers, and Transitions

**Retrieve for:** `handle_goal_or_race_change`, `review_training_block` when an event affects the objective, `recalibrate_intensity` after a credible performance, or post-race/interruption transition. It is an addendum to `topics/long_runs_and_race_development.md`, not a complete race-plan library.

## Core position

A race or benchmark is both an exposure and an observation. It may sharpen motivation, reveal current capabilities, and update intensity anchors, but it also has recovery cost and context-specific error. Add an event only when it improves the plan more than it disrupts consistency or tolerance.

Race-specific preparation should become more specific as the event becomes important and near, while broad aerobic work remains. Elite practice supports this broad sequence, but exact volumes and frequencies do not transfer to lower-volume recreational runners [E-PER-03–04].

## When a new race enters the plan

Record:

- event type, date, surface/course, and priority (`A`, `B`, `C`, or equivalent explicit meaning);
- why it matters: goal performance, practice, social event, or benchmark;
- time available and current block objective;
- prerequisites and missing race-specific exposure;
- estimated recovery/disruption cost; and
- whether the user accepts changing the active block.

Then choose one response:

1. **Keep the block; use the race as a supported exposure.** Appropriate for a lower-priority event whose demands overlap and do not require cramming.
2. **Transition the block.** Appropriate when the event is high priority, sufficiently near, and prerequisites support a focused phase.
3. **Treat as a benchmark, not a peak.** Reduce surrounding load enough for interpretable execution, but do not promise peak performance.
4. **Decline or de-prioritize.** Appropriate when the event creates unsafe density, conflicts with recovery/clinical constraints, or would replace more valuable consistency.

Do not make up missing race-specific training by stacking demanding sessions.

## Benchmarks and field tests

Use a benchmark only when it answers a decision question. Examples:

- Is a current intensity anchor materially wrong?
- Has controlled performance changed enough to transition a block?
- Is event-specific pacing/execution improving?
- Does the athlete tolerate a specific demand?

Prefer the least costly repeatable test. A race can be useful but is not automatically the best test. Record conditions, pacing, effort, symptoms, and next-day response. Compare like with like; otherwise treat it as a new observation rather than a precise trend.

### Benchmark outcomes

- **Clear improvement with stable tolerance:** consider recalibration or transition; do not increase every load dimension.
- **No clear change with adequate exposure:** audit measurement error, adherence, target selection, and recovery before changing the block.
- **Poor result with confounders:** do not recalibrate mechanically; collect a comparable observation.
- **Good result with worsening symptoms/function:** performance does not override tolerance.

## Taper principles

Tapering aims to reduce accumulated fatigue while retaining event-relevant readiness. Meta-analytic evidence supports reduced training volume while maintaining intensity and, commonly, frequency; the best duration and reduction vary by sport, training history, event, and pre-taper load [E-RACE-01]. Observational recreational-marathon data associate disciplined multiweek reductions with better outcomes, but confounding prevents a universal prescription [E-RACE-02].

Use these principles:

- reduce **volume/load**, not all movement;
- retain small doses of familiar intensity or event rhythm rather than introducing novel hard work;
- avoid unfamiliar strength, hills, surfaces, shoes, or maximal testing;
- preserve enough frequency to maintain routine when tolerated;
- protect symptom stability, sleep, fueling, and logistics;
- interpret restlessness or one poor session cautiously; and
- individualize from previous taper response and current accumulated fatigue.

Do not encode “two weeks,” “three weeks,” or a fixed percentage reduction as a universal rule. A taper cannot create missing foundational fitness.

## Event-specific planning addenda

These are decision emphases, not complete plans or prerequisites to race.

### Mile

- Emphasize efficient fast running, speed reserve, race rhythm, and tolerance of high intensity only after aerobic consistency and mechanical readiness.
- Short fast work has high neuromuscular/mechanical cost even when total duration is small.
- Race-specific sessions should remain controlled enough to protect quality and recovery; do not turn every interval into a time trial.
- A short taper may reduce volume while preserving familiar fast touches, but exact timing is individual.

### 5K

- Maintain aerobic consistency while developing threshold-oriented durability, VO2-oriented work, economy, and race-rhythm familiarity according to phase and tolerance.
- At 3-4 runs/week, one quality family may need to carry the emphasis while strides/hills remain optional small maintenance touches.
- A 5K benchmark can update broad anchors, but it does not uniquely determine threshold or mile capability.

### Half marathon

- Prioritize durable aerobic volume, long-run tolerance, threshold/steady work, fueling practice as duration warrants, and event-specific sustained running.
- Do not let race-specific pace replace basic easy volume or exceed current impact tolerance.
- A meaningful event-specific phase should follow adequate consistency; exact long-run percentage caps are unsupported.

### Marathon

- Defer a detailed marathon block until a real event, prerequisites, schedule, and health context exist.
- The main early work is durable frequency/volume, long-run progression, fueling/logistics practice, and strength compatibility.
- Marathon-specific work and taper require a much broader review than adding “marathon pace” to the current calendar.
- Finishing and performance goals may require different risk/volume decisions.

## Post-race transition

Classify the event cost from the whole response, not distance alone:

- race duration/intensity and terrain;
- musculoskeletal soreness and gait/function;
- illness, sleep, fueling, travel, and heat;
- whether the event was maximal; and
- next-day and delayed symptom response.

The next step may be easy movement, rest, cross-training, or a short consolidation period. Resume progression only after ordinary running and relevant symptoms/function are stable. Do not use a fixed number of recovery days for every event.

## Interruption and return to a block

After travel, illness, or a longer break:

1. Apply existing illness/symptom/clinical guidance.
2. Determine what exposure was lost: frequency, long-run tolerance, intensity familiarity, or all three.
3. Restore a comparable easy baseline before testing the prior peak dose.
4. Recalibrate only when evidence is comparable.
5. `continue`, `extend`, `replace`, or `end` the block; never compress missed sessions.

Detraining research supports that complete cessation can reduce cardiorespiratory and metabolic adaptations within weeks, with magnitude depending on training status, duration, and what training remains [E-TRANS-01]. It does not justify a universal day-by-day loss formula.

## Race-priority changes

A priority change is a block-review trigger, not an automatic rewrite. Preserve already approved sessions until the user accepts a versioned proposal. The proposal should state:

- what changed and why;
- what current objective is retained, reduced to maintenance, or dropped;
- what prerequisites are missing;
- what the next 14 days concretely change;
- what remains provisional; and
- the tradeoff with strength, long-run, symptom, and recovery constraints.

## What can wait until a race is real

Defer:

- a detailed week-by-week plan;
- exact pace tables and taper percentages;
- shoe, course, travel, hydration, and fueling logistics;
- event-specific long-run peaks;
- race-day pacing contingencies; and
- predicted finish time as a commitment.

The KB should retrieve or create these only when date, priority, current training, and user goal are known.

## What not to infer

- A newly entered race requires immediate race-specific intervals.
- A low-priority race must be run maximally.
- One race result uniquely identifies every training zone.
- An unsuccessful race proves the block failed.
- Elite annual volume or race-pace frequency transfers to a 3-4-run schedule.
- A taper is complete rest or a last-minute fitness block.

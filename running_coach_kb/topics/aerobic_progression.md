# Aerobic Progression and Return to Running

## Takeaways first

1. **Continuity is the main near-term objective.** A tolerable run pattern repeated across rolling 14-day cycles is more useful than chasing a weekly percentage.
2. **Do not automate the 10% rule.** The trial most often cited around graded progression found no injury reduction, and prospective evidence does not establish a universal safe increase [E-AER-01–02].
3. **Do not use ACWR as a safety gate or causal “sweet spot.”** Recent load matters, but the ratio is methodologically fragile and performed inconsistently in running cohorts [E-AER-04].
4. **Inspect individual-run novelty.** A run much longer than the recent longest run can be flagged because a large wearable cohort found an association with overuse injury; that association is not a PTT-specific cutoff or clearance line [E-AER-03].
5. **Return after illness is staged.** First exercise, full training, and full performance are different milestones. Resume only after symptoms are appropriately resolved/mild and monitor during, immediately after, and through the next day [E-AER-05–06].
6. **Progress one primary dimension at a time when possible.** Easy-run duration, long-run duration, intensity dose, hills/surface, and frequency are separate exposures.
7. **Cutback/consolidation is responsive, not calendar dogma.** Repeat or reduce a cycle when symptoms, recovery, novelty, or uncertainty justify it; no fixed every-N-weeks schedule is directly validated.

## Scope and definitions

**Training volume** can mean distance, duration, steps, or session count. Distance is convenient on flat routes; duration better captures slow/hilly conditions but still does not measure tendon force or physiological strain.

**Frequency** is the number of runs. Use the current agreed run frequency from live app state. A plan must not silently add or remove a run.

**Individual-run load** is the size and character of one exposure: distance/duration, pace or effort, hills, surface, footwear, and fatigue. It can matter even when the weekly total looks ordinary.

**Progression** is any meaningful change in total volume, longest run, faster running, terrain, surface, frequency, or mechanical stimulus such as strides/plyometrics. A “same-mileage” week can still be a progression.

**Consolidation** means holding a tolerated pattern long enough to observe recovery and symptoms. It is not failure to progress.

## What the research supports

### No universal progression percentage

**Research.** In 532 novice runners, a 13-week program described as a 10% weekly progression did not reduce running-related injuries versus an 8-week standard program: 20.8% versus 20.3% [E-AER-01; S001]. The comparison does not prove every increase is equally safe, but it directly undermines claims that the 10% rule is a validated injury-prevention threshold.

A systematic review of 36 prospective studies (23,047 runners) found conflicting associations between injuries and distance, duration, frequency, intensity, or changes in those parameters [E-AER-02; S002]. Injury definitions, exposure measurements, populations, and statistical models varied. The defensible conclusion is not “load does not matter”; it is that no universal safe increment can be selected from this literature.

**Application.** The dashboard may display percent change for context, but must not produce “safe” or “unsafe” solely from the percentage. A 5% increase with new hills and a faster long run may be more consequential than a 12% increase distributed across familiar easy routes.

### Single-session novelty deserves attention

**Research.** In a prospective cohort of 5,205 runners and 588,071 Garmin-recorded sessions, overuse-injury rates were higher after sessions categorized as more than 10% longer than the runner’s recent longest run, with adjusted hazard-rate ratios reported from 1.52 to 2.28 across spike categories. Weekly ACWR and week-to-week ratios were not useful in the same analysis [E-AER-03; S003].

**Limits.** This is an association. Injuries were self-reported; distance was the exposure; categories and lookback were investigator choices; harder terrain, intent, prior symptoms, or behavior may confound the result. It did not validate a PTT-specific cutoff.

**Application.** Use a “novel individual run” flag with context and uncertainty. A planned run just below a category boundary is not cleared, and one just above is not automatically prohibited. Compare duration as well as distance when pace or terrain differs.

### ACWR, monotony, and strain

**Research.** ACWR divides a recent “acute” load by a longer “chronic” load. Critiques show that mathematical coupling, arbitrary windows, treatment of zero/missing load, and ratio behavior can create artifacts; no causal evidence establishes that moving an athlete into a “sweet spot” prevents injury [E-AER-04; S004]. A 2026 recreational-running cohort found a small duration-based association that disappeared after excluding runners injured at baseline, with limited clinical relevance [S005].

**Monotony/strain.** Foster-style monotony typically relates mean daily load to its day-to-day variation, and strain multiplies weekly load by monotony. These can describe a schedule, but direct evidence that a specific score predicts or prevents running injury for this athlete is insufficient. Rest days and the choice of load unit can strongly affect the number.

**Application.** These metrics can be descriptive dashboard features only. Never use a green ACWR/monotony value to overrule worsening symptoms or a red value to cancel training automatically.

### Return after illness

**Clinical guidance.** IOC guidance for selected mild acute respiratory infections supports beginning with a controlled exercise challenge after symptoms are very mild/resolved and no red flags exist, then monitoring during, immediately after, and for 24 hours. It gives an example of 10–20 minutes at roughly 60–70% of usual intensity [E-AER-05; S006]. That number is consensus guidance for selected cases, not a universal prescription.

**Research.** A systematic review found heterogeneous illness courses and limited return-to-sport evidence [S007]. In the 2026 AWARE X cohort, median time to first training, full training, and full performance differed (3.5, 8, and 11 days), with wide variation by severity and pathogen [E-AER-06; S008].

**Application.** Do not restore the previous long run or quality session on the first day back. The first session is a test, not proof of full capacity. Missed sessions are not repaid. Cardiopulmonary/systemic symptoms, moderate/severe illness, or clinician restrictions require a different path.

## Time versus distance

**Research interpretation.** Neither unit is universally superior. Distance maps to impacts/steps only imperfectly; duration better reflects time exposed but can hide faster speed. The app should store both when available.

**Inference for this athlete.** Prefer duration to communicate easy-run growth when terrain/pace varies, but track distance and elevation as mechanical context. For the long run, compare both time and distance with the recent longest comparable exposure. Never convert cycling minutes into equivalent running miles.

## Frequency versus run duration

At low-to-moderate volume, adding frequency can distribute mileage into smaller exposures, but it also adds a new impact day and changes the established routine. Direct evidence defining the better option for injury prevention is insufficient [E-AER-02]. The agent should progress within the currently agreed cadence unless the user chooses otherwise.

**Inference.** With frequency fixed, distribute aerobic growth preferentially through small changes to ordinary easy runs before allowing the long run to absorb most of the total. This is a transparency/robustness principle, not a validated percentage cap.

## How to propose the next 14-day segment

1. Reconstruct what was actually completed.
2. Identify symptoms during, shortly after, next morning, and later each mechanically important session.
3. Check illness, recovery, and strength interference.
4. Flag novel individual runs, hills/surface changes, and simultaneous progressions.
5. Choose one target:
   - repeat/consolidate;
   - add a small amount to ordinary easy running;
   - extend the long run while other load is stable;
   - progress one quality dose while volume is stable;
   - restore training after illness;
   - reduce running load and use tolerated cross-training.
6. State what observation will validate or reject the change.

The size of the change remains **null** until calibrated from actual data and symptom response.

## Cutback weeks

**Coaching convention.** Scheduled cutbacks are common because they create recovery and planning opportunities. The review did not identify a universal cadence or percentage validated for injury prevention.

**Operational policy.** Use a consolidation/reduction segment when one or more of these occur: delayed symptoms are less predictable, a run was novel, quality declines, strength soreness repeatedly interferes, illness has occurred, life stress rises, or several load dimensions changed. A planned cutback may also be reasonable for logistics. Do not force a reduction solely because a calendar count is reached.

## What one runner’s history cannot prove

- Tolerating a higher weekly volume in the past does not prove an immediate return is safe after illness or symptoms.
- One symptom-free long run does not establish tolerance when responses can be delayed.
- One injury-free progression does not validate the progression rule.
- A faster pace at the same heart rate does not establish fitness gain unless conditions and sensor quality are comparable.
- A flare after one run does not identify a single causal variable when pace, distance, hills, lifting, sleep, and footwear changed together.

## Decision cues for the agent

**Proceed/consider progression** when the rolling 14-day plan was substantially completed, walking and shorter runs remain stable, delayed response is repeatably acceptable, recovery is normal, and the proposed change is isolated.

**Consolidate** when data are missing, the prior cycle contained novelty, or adaptation is favorable but tendon response is not yet known.

**Reduce/substitute** when delayed symptoms worsen, daily function declines, recovery deteriorates, or the long run/quality/strength cluster is too dense.

**Seek clinical input** when symptoms are progressive, function-limiting, diagnostically unclear, or not settling with reasonable modification.

## Relevant inputs

- Rolling 14-day planned/completed run count
- Distance and duration by run
- Longest comparable run in recent history
- Session goal, RPE, terrain, elevation, surface, and weather
- PTT symptoms during and at delayed checkpoints
- Strength session timing and lower-body novelty/soreness
- Illness course and current symptoms
- Missingness and data-quality flags

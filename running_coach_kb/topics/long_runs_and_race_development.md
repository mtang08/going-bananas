# Long Runs and Race Development

## Takeaways first

1. **Long runs are valuable but not governed by a validated universal percentage cap.** Performance associations exist; individual safe doses do not [E-LONG-01–02].
2. **At low weekly volume, the long run can dominate mechanical load even at easy intensity.** Evaluate duration, recent longest run, terrain, effort, and delayed symptoms.
3. **A reported long-run distance and pace are observations, not an established easy pace.** Effort, HR quality, terrain, weather, and fueling are unknown.
4. **Progress the long run only after shorter running is stable.** Avoid increasing long-run size while also adding intensity, hills, plyometrics, or new lower-body strength load.
5. **Cross-training can support aerobic work when running is constrained, but it is not one-for-one running durability** [E-LONG-03].
6. **Half/marathon foundations over the next 1–2 years are continuity, broad aerobic capacity, strength, tissue tolerance, and fueling practice—not an early marathon block.**
7. **Mile/5K goals are compatible with marathon foundations.** General aerobic development and strength overlap; race-specific peaks should be sequenced rather than stacked.

## What a long run is

There is no universal duration or percentage definition. Operationally, a long run is the run that creates the largest sustained running exposure in the cycle. Its load has at least four dimensions:

- **Physiological:** duration and effort relative to thresholds
- **Mechanical:** steps, terrain, surface, footwear, speed, and fatigue-related form changes
- **Metabolic:** carbohydrate availability, hydration, and duration
- **Recovery:** delayed symptoms, soreness, sleep, and effect on the next run/lift

A low-intensity long run can be mechanically demanding. Conversely, a moderately long but fast/hilly run may be both physiologically and mechanically demanding.

## Evidence for performance and dose

**Research.** Observational work associates higher weekly volume and longer endurance runs with better half-marathon/marathon performance [E-LONG-01; S020–S021]. One prospective cohort of 997 recreational runners did not find a clear injury association for the long-run parameters studied, but healthy-runner bias, self-selection, and exposure limitations prevent treating that as proof of safety.

**No direct threshold.** The review did not locate a trial validating a rule such as “the long run must be no more than X% of weekly mileage” for injury prevention [E-LONG-02]. Such caps are coaching conventions that can still prompt useful scrutiny, especially at low volume, but the percentage changes dramatically with run frequency and does not account for terrain, effort, or history.

**Application.** The app should show the long run’s share of 14-day volume descriptively and separately flag novelty relative to the recent longest comparable run. Neither number clears or forbids the session.

## Progressing the long run

### Preconditions

- Stable walking/daily function
- Stable response to shorter easy runs
- Known response to the previous long run through the delayed later window
- No active illness return problem
- No unresolved collision with lower-body strength or quality work
- Adequate practical fueling/hydration for expected duration

### One-change principle

**Inference.** When long-run duration grows, hold its intensity, terrain, and the formal quality dose broadly stable. If a route change adds hills/trails or pace rises, treat that as part of the progression.

### Progression size

No universal number is supported. The future agent should propose a change only after inspecting repeated tolerance. Possible actions are:

- repeat the last tolerated duration;
- extend slightly while using the same route/effort;
- shorten but add low-impact aerobic time;
- distribute more aerobic duration to ordinary easy runs;
- postpone growth while maintaining continuity.

The numeric increment remains `null` until calibrated from live data.

### Delayed response

Some runners report tenderness later after a long run. Therefore the long run cannot be declared tolerated at finish. The weekly workflow must link later observations to the exposure and avoid scheduling the next major progression before that window is known.

## Distribution across runs

**Evidence gap.** No high-quality evidence identifies one ideal share of total mileage per run for this population. More even distribution may reduce single-session novelty, while a dedicated long run develops sustained tolerance. Frequency itself is fixed by preference.

**Inference.** At the agreed rolling-cycle run count, do not let nearly all volume growth accrue to the long run. Small growth in ordinary easy runs can improve total aerobic stimulus while keeping the longest exposure observable. This is not a percentage rule.

## Long run and the quality session

During a tendon-limited rebuild, a quality session plus a long run each week can be reasonable only if:

- quality is controlled rather than exhaustive;
- long-run effort remains controlled;
- the sessions and lower-body lifts are spaced in a way the athlete tolerates;
- symptoms and recovery remain stable.

A “fast long run” can become the second quality session. Without effort context, classify the run as **unknown intensity** and count it conservatively in the weekly review [P010].

## Half-marathon development

For a half-marathon goal, do not produce a race plan until the event/date, current training, and thresholds are confirmed. Near-term foundations are:

- consistent agreed rolling-cycle rhythm;
- gradually greater total aerobic duration;
- a controlled long run that does not provoke worsening delayed symptoms;
- one quality exposure chosen for the phase;
- strength preserved;
- simple fueling practice on longer sessions;
- periodic standardized fitness checks rather than Garmin prediction alone.

**Inference.** A recent 5K result alone cannot establish half-marathon endurance readiness, injury tolerance, or race outcome. Do not turn it into a prediction.

## Marathon foundations for a future goal

A marathon-specific block years early is unnecessary and may be counterproductive. Useful preparation over 1–2 years includes:

1. **Continuity:** months of repeatable training with few symptom-forced gaps.
2. **Volume tolerance:** gradual increase distributed across runs, not only the long run.
3. **Long-run durability:** increasing sustained time on feet with stable delayed response.
4. **Strength:** continued meaningful full-body lifting; later adjust dose around peak run load rather than eliminate it.
5. **Fueling skills:** learn pre-run, during-run, and recovery strategies without mandatory calorie tracking.
6. **Terrain and footwear familiarity:** introduced separately from large volume changes.
7. **Race experience:** shorter races and a half marathon can test pacing, logistics, and recovery.
8. **Health:** clinical evaluation when symptoms remain progressive or diagnosis is uncertain.

Entry and time goal are unconfirmed; do not presume a qualifying or time target.

## Mile and 5K goals alongside endurance

Faster mile and 5K goals require speed qualities, but these can be sequenced:

- **Now:** aerobic continuity, strength, controlled quality, optional calibrated strides when tolerated.
- **Later 5K phase:** threshold plus periodic VO2/race-pace work, while preserving the long run at a tolerable level.
- **Later mile emphasis:** greater neuromuscular and high-speed specificity, with volume/intensity tradeoffs and strong tissue prerequisites.
- **Marathon foundation throughout:** mostly easy aerobic work and durability do not disappear during shorter-distance phases.

This mixed long-term approach avoids trying to peak all goals at once.

## Cross-training

**Research.** A small cycling-substitution trial maintained estimated VO2max over five weeks; a 2026 meta-analysis of seven randomized trials found no clear superiority between cross-training and running comparators, with wide uncertainty [E-LONG-03; S022–S023].

**Role.** Use cycling, elliptical, pool running, or another tolerated mode when running load is constrained but aerobic exercise remains appropriate. The mode must be calibrated separately.

**Limits.** Cross-training cannot be converted one-for-one into running miles, does not guarantee running economy or tendon durability, and can interfere with strength through local fatigue. Elliptical or cycling may still provoke the symptomatic region.

## Fueling the long run

Quality and longer sessions are more likely than short easy runs to benefit from deliberate carbohydrate availability [E-FUEL-02]. The package does not prescribe grams without duration, body size, dietary context, and GI tolerance. The agent can ask:

- Was the athlete able to eat normally before the session?
- Was the planned duration long enough to merit carrying fluid/carbohydrate?
- Did energy, GI symptoms, or late-run RPE deteriorate?

Do not infer underfueling from Garmin calorie estimates.

## Monitoring and meaningful comparisons

Track:

- distance, duration, and elapsed versus moving time;
- route, elevation, surface, weather;
- intended effort, session RPE, talk-test notes;
- HR signal quality and drift only when comparable;
- fueling/hydration context;
- PTT symptoms during, after, next morning, and a later follow-up window;
- next strength/run quality.

No universal aerobic-decoupling cutoff predicts readiness or injury [E-DATA-02]. Use it as a descriptive trend only under standardized conditions.

## Decision cues

**Repeat:** prior long run was completed but delayed response is unknown or the cycle added another stimulus.

**Extend:** repeated stable response, normal recovery, controlled effort, and no simultaneous major progression.

**Shorten/flatten:** symptoms, hills, heat, effort, or lifting proximity made the prior exposure more costly.

**Substitute:** running is mechanically constrained but low-impact aerobic work is tolerated.

**Seek clinical input:** walking/function worsens, symptoms progress, or repeated modifications do not restore stable tolerance.

## Relevant inputs

- Actual 6–12-week running history
- Recent longest comparable run
- Long-run duration, distance, route, elevation, surface, weather
- Effort/RPE/HR quality and fueling
- Delayed symptom series
- Quality-session and strength placement
- Event confirmation and date

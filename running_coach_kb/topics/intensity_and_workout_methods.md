# Intensity Distribution and Workout Methods

## Takeaways first

1. **No distribution model has established universal superiority.** Polarized and pyramidal approaches both work; recreational runners may do at least as well with pyramidal training [E-INT-01–03].
2. **Do not enforce 80/20.** Seven runs per fortnight makes session-count percentages unstable, and time-in-zone depends on incompatible zone definitions and warm-up/recovery accounting [E-INT-04].
3. **Predominantly easy running is the practical rebuild default, not a magic ratio.** One formal quality run plus one controlled long run per week is already two potentially demanding exposures, before strength is counted.
4. **A long run can be demanding at low intensity.** Classify physiological and mechanical cost separately.
5. **Threshold, VO2, strides, and hills are methods with different purposes and costs.** Select the least complex method that serves the current phase; do not stack them.
6. **Norwegian double threshold is elite practice, not a default solution.** Acute data do not demonstrate long-term superiority, and lactate-controlled elite doses do not transfer [E-INT-06].
7. **“Zone 2” must name its definition.** Garmin five-zone Zone 2, a three-zone low-intensity domain, and work just below LT1/VT1 are not automatically equivalent [E-INT-05].
8. **Mile/5K goals can coexist with aerobic priority.** Maintain or later introduce a small calibrated speed exposure, then concentrate race-specific work only after continuity and tendon tolerance are stable.

## Intensity models and classification

### Two-zone model

Often divides training below versus above a threshold. Which threshold matters: first ventilatory/lactate threshold, second threshold, critical speed, or another boundary. Calling both sides “easy/hard” hides the heavy domain.

### Three-zone model

Commonly defines:

- Zone 1: below first lactate/ventilatory threshold
- Zone 2: between first and second thresholds
- Zone 3: above second threshold

Polarized and pyramidal research often uses this model. It does not map one-to-one to Garmin’s five zones.

### Five-zone model

Common in watches and coaching systems. Boundaries may use percentages of max HR, HR reserve, threshold HR, pace, or power. The label “Zone 2” depends on the setup.

### Session-goal versus time-in-zone

A workout with hard repetitions may spend much of its recorded time at low HR because HR lags and recovery/warm-up count. Session-goal classification may call it hard; time-in-zone classification may call it mostly easy. Both describe different things [E-INT-04; S012–S013].

**Dashboard requirement.** Store raw duration, HR/pace/RPE, session intent, and classification method. Never show a distribution percentage without naming its model and denominator.

## Comparing distributions

### Predominantly easy / pyramidal

Most time is easy, with smaller moderate and high-intensity shares. The 2025 individual-participant network meta-analysis found no overall polarized-versus-pyramidal advantage, and subgroup results suggested recreational athletes may respond at least as well to pyramidal training [E-INT-02; S010].

**Application.** This is the simplest descriptive pattern for a low-volume rebuild, but it need not be engineered from percentages. One controlled threshold-like session can create a pyramidal pattern naturally.

### Polarized

Large easy share, little threshold/middle work, and a small high-intensity share. In a 10-week randomized trial of 30 recreational runners, both polarized and between-threshold groups improved, and the primary between-group difference was not significant [E-INT-01; S009]. A 2024 meta-analysis found a small VO2peak effect but no clear advantage for several performance outcomes [E-INT-03; S011].

**Application.** Polarization may be a valid later choice, especially when volume is sufficient to support hard/easy separation. It is not a reason to introduce very hard work during a fragile rebuild.

### Threshold-oriented

Emphasizes work near a sustainable heavy-intensity boundary. It may improve performance but can accumulate substantial medium-hard fatigue. Definitions differ, and without validated thresholds the coach should anchor to controlled RPE/talk/rep stability rather than a borrowed lactate number.

**Application.** A controlled interval session is a plausible quality option because recoveries can limit drift. It should not become a second formal quality day.

### Predominantly easy / all-easy phase

Useful during illness return, symptom uncertainty, or consolidation. The absence of intensity is not an optimal long-term plan for every goal, but it may be the highest-value temporary choice.

## Two demanding exposures within the agreed rolling-cycle run count

The current pattern contains approximately two quality sessions and two long runs per 14 days. That is not automatically excessive, but “two hard sessions per week” language is misleading because:

- a long run may be low intensity yet mechanically demanding;
- one quality session may be controlled threshold or high-cost VO2 work;
- lower-body strength adds neuromuscular/mechanical load;
- the currently agreed running cadence limits placement options;
- PTT symptoms are delayed.

**Inference.** During the rebuild, retain one formal quality and one controlled long run per week only when the long run remains controlled and the delayed response is stable. If the long run itself becomes fast, hilly, novel, or symptom-provoking, modify it or the quality exposure rather than forcing both.

## Workout methods

### Controlled threshold intervals

**Purpose.** Improve sustainable aerobic speed and efficiency around the heavy domain.

**Why/when.** Useful when the athlete can complete easy/long running reliably and wants a moderate-cost quality stimulus. Intervals can constrain duration and make form/effort easier to monitor than a long tempo.

**Dose evidence.** Protocols vary too much to prescribe one set. The reviewed recreational trial supports effectiveness of between-threshold training, not a universal format [S009].

**Costs.** Faster mechanics and glycogen demand; can become a disguised race effort.

**Decision cues.** Stable PTT response; normal warm-up; repeatable RPE; enough recovery from lower-body lifting; deliberate fueling.

**Alternatives.** Fartlek, short aerobic intervals, all-easy consolidation.

### Multi-minute VO2 intervals

**Purpose.** Increase high-end aerobic power and time near high oxygen uptake.

**Evidence.** Several interval forms improve fitness. One high-dose 4 × 4-minute program reported ~6.5% VO2max improvement, but network evidence does not establish a universal winner and many trials use two to three hard sessions weekly [E-INT-07; S017–S019].

**Role.** Later and phase-specific for mile/5K development, not the current aerobic rebuild priority.

**Costs.** High cardiometabolic fatigue and faster running mechanics; may interfere with strength or long-run recovery.

**Monitoring.** Rep stability, form, RPE, next-day and later PTT response, and effect on lifting.

### Short aerobic intervals / 10–20–30-style work

**Purpose.** Accumulate pace changes with short bouts and potentially lower perceived burden.

**Evidence/application.** Small trials suggest benefit, but fast segments and repeated accelerations can be mechanically demanding. “Short” is not synonymous with tendon-safe.

**Role.** Potential later rotation after controlled calibration; avoid all-out sprinting.

### Relaxed strides

**Purpose.** Preserve speed familiarity, coordination, and running economy qualities with minimal metabolic load.

**Evidence.** Direct evidence for a minimum effective dose or injury benefit in tendon-limited recreational runners is insufficient [E-INT-08].

**Inference.** May be a small bridge between aerobic priority and mile/5K goals once symptoms are stable. Use smooth, non-exhaustive acceleration, full recovery, and movement quality rather than watch pace.

**Costs.** Peak speed and acceleration still load the calf/foot.

### Hills and hill sprints

**Purpose.** Force/technique stimulus at lower absolute speed.

**Evidence/application.** No reviewed evidence validates a PTT-safe dose. Uphill running can increase plantarflexor demand; maximal hill sprints are a separate high-impact progression.

**Role.** Later only, after flat faster running and calf/foot loading are tolerated. Do not add hills while increasing volume or plyometrics.

### All-out sprint intervals

**Purpose.** Time-efficient high-intensity stimulus.

**Role now.** None. They are not required for aerobic development or the stated goals and create high acceleration/neuromuscular cost.

## Norwegian singles and double threshold

**Research.** Best-practice studies describe elite systems. An acute crossover in 14 elite men found some lower drift/fatigue markers when 60 minutes of threshold work was split into two sessions, but this does not establish long-term superiority [E-INT-06; S015–S016].

**Why it does not transfer now.** Elite volume, lactate testing, fueling, recovery time, and tissue tolerance may be absent in a recreational runner with constrained scheduling and tendon symptoms.

**Policy.** No lactate target, threshold volume, or double-session pattern should be imported. One controlled quality session is simpler, more observable, and adequate.

## “Zone 2” without mythology

An expert panel preferred defining Zone 2 as work immediately below the first lactate/ventilatory threshold, while emphasizing that adaptations are not unique to a narrow intensity [E-INT-05; S014]. Internet usage often means Garmin HR Zone 2 or merely easy running.

**Operational rule.** The app must show:

- zone model and calculation basis;
- threshold estimate and date, if any;
- whether the value is device default, field test, lab test, or user-set;
- talk-test/RPE context.

Without that, prescribe “conversational controlled effort,” not “Zone 2.”

## Block versus mixed periodization

A low-quality systematic review found small pooled effects favoring block periodization for VO2max/maximal power in trained athletes [E-PER-01; S056]. This is insufficient to justify concentrated blocks during a tendon-limited rebuild.

**Inference.** Mixed training with a single current emphasis is more suitable: mostly easy aerobic work, one controlled quality exposure, long-run development, and two lifts. Later, a mile/5K or half-marathon phase can shift the quality emphasis without abandoning aerobic continuity or strength.

## Mile/5K compatibility with aerobic priority

The goals need not be pursued simultaneously at peak specificity. Over the next 3–6 months:

- aerobic continuity and tolerable volume support all distances;
- strength remains meaningful;
- a small calibrated speed exposure may preserve familiarity when symptoms allow;
- formal VO2/race-pace work can rotate in later phases;
- faster mile and 5K goals should not force high-intensity volume.

This is a sequencing inference, not evidence that one exact order is optimal.

## Decision cues

**Choose threshold-like work** when the phase needs sustainable aerobic speed, the tendon is stable, and lifting/long-run placement permits recovery.

**Choose VO2 work** later when high-end aerobic power is the explicit phase target and stable faster-running tolerance exists.

**Choose strides** as optional low-volume speed familiarity only when acceleration/deceleration is tolerated.

**Choose all-easy** after illness, with uncertain symptoms, or when several demanding exposures collide.

**Do not choose a method** because the distribution graph or Garmin label looks wrong.

## Relevant inputs

- Current phase and next confirmed event
- Run frequency and rolling 14-day session map
- Session intent, RPE, pace/HR source, and zone model
- Long-run duration/effort and delayed symptoms
- Strength timing, lower-body soreness, explosive work
- Surface, hills, weather, and sensor quality
- Fueling preparedness and recovery trend

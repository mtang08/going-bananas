# Fueling, Energy Availability, and Concurrent Training

## Takeaways first

1. **Fueling should scale with demand.** Quality, long, and combined run-lift days are more likely to need deliberate carbohydrate support than short easy runs [E-FUEL-02].
2. **Do not require calorie tracking.** The future coach can use brief preparedness and recovery questions, meal timing, and optional experiments.
3. **Do not infer energy availability from Garmin calorie estimates.** Wearable expenditure plus unmeasured intake cannot diagnose low energy availability or REDs [E-FUEL-01].
4. **REDs is a clinical, multifactorial syndrome.** Persistent fatigue, recurrent illness/injury, bone or endocrine concerns, or disclosed eating/weight issues warrant discussion/clinical support—not an automated label.
5. **Protein supports resistance adaptation.** Supplementation has small average benefits when total intake is otherwise lower; regular protein-containing meals are a practical default [E-FUEL-03].
6. **Hybrid training makes recovery context important.** A run may be underfueled even when total weekly intake is adequate, and a lift may suffer after a long/quality run.
7. **Exact grams/calories remain optional and individualized.** The package does not know body size, dietary goals, GI tolerance, or medical context.

## Definitions

### Fueling

Food and fluid consumed before, during, and after a session to support performance, comfort, and recovery.

### Energy availability

Dietary energy remaining for physiological functions after exercise energy expenditure, often normalized to fat-free mass in research. It is not the same as energy balance, and accurate field measurement is difficult.

### Low energy availability and REDs

Low energy availability can be short-term/adaptive or problematic. Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) is a broader clinical framework involving health and performance effects across multiple systems [E-FUEL-01; S052]. It cannot be diagnosed from fatigue, weight, a watch, or one dietary recall.

## Energy availability evidence

**Clinical consensus/research.** The IOC REDs consensus and broader literature associate problematic low energy availability with health/performance consequences, including bone, endocrine, immune, and performance concerns, but prevalence and thresholds vary by population and method [E-FUEL-01; S052, S055].

**Application.** The app should recognize underfueling as one possible confounder when fatigue persists, but must not diagnose. It should never compare Garmin “calories burned” with a guessed intake.

### Brief non-diagnostic screening in coaching

For an unexpectedly difficult session, ask one or two questions:

- Were you able to eat normally in the hours before this session?
- Was there a long gap since the last meal/snack?
- Did energy fade, hunger become distracting, or GI issues limit intake?
- Have several demanding sessions occurred with poor appetite or limited recovery meals?

Persistent concerns or health signs should prompt a sports dietitian/clinician, especially when combined with recurrent injury/illness, bone concerns, endocrine/menstrual changes if relevant/disclosed, substantial unintentional weight change, or restrictive eating.

## Carbohydrate support

**Research/position guidance.** Sports nutrition guidance recommends matching carbohydrate availability to training demand rather than applying one intake to every day [E-FUEL-02; S053]. Exact amounts depend on session duration/intensity, total training, body size, goals, diet, and GI tolerance.

### Short easy sessions

Many can be completed comfortably with normal meals and hydration. Fasted training is not required for aerobic adaptation and should not be defaulted by the agent.

### Quality sessions

Carbohydrate availability can help the athlete achieve the intended quality and reduce the chance that an “easy modification” is actually an underfueled failure. The practical question is whether the athlete begins prepared, not whether a watch predicts enough glycogen.

### Long runs

As duration grows, pre-run eating, carrying fluid/carbohydrate, and practicing tolerance become part of event preparation. The exact point and dose remain individualized. Track GI response, perceived energy, late-run RPE, and recovery.

### Double-session or run-lift days

The interval between sessions creates a recovery/fueling challenge. Carbohydrate plus a protein-containing meal/snack may support the second session, but exact prescription depends on separation and goals. If the second session repeatedly underperforms, review both scheduling and fueling.

## Protein and strength recovery

**Research.** A meta-analysis of 49 resistance-training trials (1,863 adults) found protein supplementation produced small average gains in strength (~2.49 kg in pooled testing) and fat-free mass (~0.30 kg), with a plateau estimate near 1.62 g/kg/day in that research context [E-FUEL-03; S054].

**Limits.** This is not a direct prescription for this athlete or a concurrent run-lift trial. Baseline intake, energy sufficiency, training program, age, and supplement type matter.

**Application.** Encourage regular protein-containing meals distributed across the day and a recovery meal after demanding training. Supplements are optional convenience, not required. Exact grams are only supplied if the athlete requests a nutrition-specific plan and relevant context is known.

## Hydration and environment

Fluid needs vary with temperature, humidity, duration, sweat rate, and access. Dehydration and heat can raise HR and RPE, confounding pace/HR trends. The app should store weather and note whether fluid access changed. It should not generate a universal fluid/sodium dose without sweat and medical context.

## Fueling as a fatigue confounder

When a quality session is unexpectedly poor, consider:

- illness or accumulated fatigue;
- strength soreness;
- heat/humidity;
- inadequate pre-session food/fluid;
- unusually long gap since eating;
- GI distress;
- bad pacing or sensor error.

Do not select one cause without evidence. A successful snack experiment may improve practice but does not diagnose chronic low energy availability.

## Body composition and weight

No body-composition goal is confirmed. The coach should not propose intentional weight loss, infer an “ideal racing weight,” or use watch calories to create a deficit. Performance and tendon health can be harmed by aggressive restriction; any weight-oriented plan should be explicit, voluntary, and appropriately supported.

## Practical low-friction prompts

### Before a quality/long run

- “Prepared to train?” yes / partly / no
- Optional reason: food, fluid, sleep, illness, stress, GI

### After

- Energy: steady / faded / unknown
- GI: fine / issue
- Recovery meal possible: yes / delayed

These fields are optional and should appear mainly for demanding/long sessions, not every short easy run.

## When to modify training

**Proceed** when the athlete feels prepared and no other concern exists.

**Modify** when a demanding session begins clearly underprepared; choose easy running, shorter work, rescheduling, or a small snack/delay when appropriate and safe.

**Review pattern** when several sessions show energy fade, poor recovery, or unusual hunger alongside rising load.

**Refer** when persistent fatigue combines with concerning health, injury, dietary, or weight signs; the app does not diagnose REDs.

## Interaction with tendon and illness

Adequate energy/protein supports general recovery, but no food strategy guarantees tendon healing or prevents injury. Illness symptoms are not to be “fueled through.” Persistent tendon symptoms despite adequate recovery still need load/clinical assessment.

## Relevant inputs

- Session type, duration, intensity, and time of day
- Time since last meal/snack and subjective preparedness
- During-session intake for longer work, if any
- GI tolerance and energy fade
- Recovery meal timing
- Current dietary restrictions/preferences if volunteered
- Training/life stress and illness
- Clinician/dietitian instructions

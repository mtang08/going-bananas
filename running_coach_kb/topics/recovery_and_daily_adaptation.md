# Recovery and Daily Adaptation

## Takeaways first

1. **One ordinary poor night is not an automatic rest day.** Severe sleep loss impairs performance on average, but evidence is less certain for a single modestly suboptimal night [E-REC-01].
2. **Accumulated deterioration matters more than a single noisy score.** Several agreeing days of fatigue, poor sleep, unusual resting physiology, soreness, or performance decline justify modifying the next demanding exposure.
3. **Subjective feedback is a first-class signal.** A 10–20-second check-in can add information that wearables do not capture [E-REC-02].
4. **HRV can contextualize training but should not veto a session from one night.** HRV-guided approaches show small/inconsistent superiority, and study protocols differ from Garmin’s overnight metrics [E-REC-03].
5. **Garmin Training Readiness is a proprietary composite, not independently validated clearance.** Expose components and conflicts; symptoms outrank the score [E-WEAR-05].
6. **Proceed/modify/rest decisions should combine symptoms, function, illness, recovery trends, plan context, and warm-up response.** No universal algorithm is established [E-REC-04].
7. **Do not label unexplained fatigue “overtraining” or “underfueling.”** Consider illness, training, life stress, sleep, nutrition, sensor error, and other causes.

## Sleep evidence and interpretation

### Sleep duration and quality

**Research.** Athlete sleep consensus emphasizes that sleep need and vulnerability vary. Laboratory sleep loss and deprivation generally impair endurance and other performance outcomes; one meta-analysis reported an endurance effect around SMD -0.52 [E-REC-01; S037–S038].

**Limits.** Laboratory protocols often involve total deprivation or more severe restriction than a real-world “bad night.” Evidence for one night that is merely shorter or lower quality than usual is less precise. Sleep opportunity, actual sleep, perceived quality, and device score are distinct.

### One poor night

**Inference.** When illness, PTT symptoms, perceived fatigue, and recent recovery are normal, retain an easy run and reduce optional ambition rather than cancel automatically. For a quality session, use the warm-up and offer an easy fallback. Avoid maximal tests.

### Accumulated restriction

Several poor nights combined with fatigue, unusual RPE, mood changes, or resting-physiology changes are more persuasive. Modify the next demanding exposure first, then review total training and life stress rather than immediately rewriting the full plan.

### Consumer sleep scores

Wearables tend to estimate sleep timing/duration better than stages, and accuracy is device/firmware dependent [E-WEAR-02]. Deep/REM minutes and a composite score should not be treated as precise physiological measurements.

## Subjective monitoring

**Research.** In a small five-week trial of 36 male recreational runners, subjective-feedback-guided training produced favorable improvements relative to predefined and HRV-guided groups, but group sizes were tiny and bias/replication concerns are substantial [E-REC-02; S041].

**Application.** The finding supports—not proves—the value of a brief check-in. Subjective data should be structured enough for trends but low friction.

### Recommended 10–20-second daily check-in

1. **PTT today:** normal/stable, better, worse, or unknown.
2. **Delayed response since last run:** none/usual, worse, or unknown.
3. **Fatigue/illness:** normal, elevated, or ill/concerning.
4. **Interfering lower-body soreness:** none, mild/noninterfering, or meaningful.
5. Optional after-session RPE: one value plus “as expected / harder / easier.”

No field should default to normal when skipped.

## HRV

### What it measures

Heart-rate variability summarizes beat-to-beat variation, commonly RMSSD at rest/overnight. It is influenced by autonomic state, sleep, illness, training, alcohol, stress, breathing, measurement position/time, and artifact.

### Evidence for guided training

Meta-analytic and small randomized evidence suggests HRV-guided endurance training may reduce unnecessary hard sessions and produce modest benefits in some outcomes, but pooled superiority is small or nonsignificant and protocols differ [E-REC-03; S039–S042]. Study systems often use standardized morning measurements and rolling smallest-worthwhile-change rules, not Garmin Training Readiness.

### Operational use

- Prefer an individualized multi-day trend from the same device and routine.
- Never compare absolute HRV across devices/people.
- Do not use one low night as a veto.
- Give more weight when HRV deterioration agrees with subjective fatigue, sleep, RHR, illness, or performance.
- Treat a favorable HRV as non-clearance when symptoms worsen.

No universal HRV threshold is activated.

## Resting heart rate

RHR can rise with illness, heat, stress, dehydration, poor sleep, or training strain, but individual variation and device conditions matter. Use trend and agreement with other signals. There is no package-level “+X bpm” cutoff because baseline, measurement method, and false positives differ.

## Fatigue, soreness, and performance response

### Fatigue

Distinguish:

- general sleepiness;
- heavy legs after lifting;
- systemic malaise/illness;
- local tendon symptoms;
- motivational stress;
- unexpectedly high effort at a known workload.

The action differs. The dashboard cannot diagnose overreaching or overtraining from a short trend.

### Soreness

Diffuse, expected muscle soreness may permit easy training if gait and coordination are normal. Focal, sharp, progressive, swelling-associated, or function-changing symptoms are not treated as routine DOMS.

### Warm-up response

A controlled warm-up is a useful decision point when no red flag exists. Improvement can support a modified session; worsening effort, coordination, or pain supports conversion to easy/rest. Warm-up response does not erase delayed tendon monitoring.

## Readiness composites

Garmin states that Training Readiness combines sleep score, recovery time, HRV status, acute load, sleep history, and stress [E-WEAR-05; S048]. This describes the product; it does not validate a decision threshold or causal benefit.

**App requirement:** display component values, timestamps, and conflicts. Example: “Readiness high, but delayed PTT response worse” should result in symptom-led modification.

## Proceed / modify / rest framework

### Proceed

- Symptoms/function stable
- No concerning illness signal
- Recovery broadly normal or only one isolated poor sleep night
- Planned exposure resembles tolerated work
- No major scheduling conflict

Use normal session with a conservative option when confidence is moderate.

### Modify

- Several recovery signals worsen
- Strength soreness changes movement or target quality
- Session is novel relative to recent history
- Delayed tendon response is uncertain/worse
- Heat/hills/sensor issues make target pace inappropriate

Modify the smallest variable that preserves intent: duration, intensity, terrain, placement, or mode.

### Rest

- Systemic illness/recovery makes training inappropriate
- Even the fallback is unlikely to be useful
- Current symptoms/function are deteriorating and impact work should pause pending assessment
- A deliberate rest day protects the next priority session better than adding low-quality work

### Seek clinical input

Use when illness or musculoskeletal patterns fall outside routine adaptation or P018 applies.

## Avoiding overreaction and neglect

### Overreaction failure modes

- Canceling easy runs for one low HRV or sleep score
- Treating watch recovery time as a prohibition
- Rewriting the week after one slow hot/hilly run
- Adding rest without considering that consistent training itself supports sleep/routine

### Neglect failure modes

- Ignoring several days of fatigue because one score is green
- Using good readiness to override worsening pain
- Repeating a provoking long run before the delayed response is known
- Dismissing illness or cardiopulmonary symptoms as poor motivation

## Missing/stale data

A missing HRV value is not low HRV; a missing check-in is not normal symptoms. The decision can still use plan, function, last exposure, and other data. State the missing field and reduce confidence. Ask only when it blocks safety or session classification.

## Fueling as a recovery confounder

Quality, long, and combined run-lift days can feel disproportionately difficult when carbohydrate availability or overall intake is inadequate. Ask a brief preparedness question without diagnosing low energy availability or requiring calorie tracking [E-FUEL-01–03].

## Relevant inputs

- Sleep duration/quality trend and source
- Subjective fatigue, illness, mood/stress if volunteered
- HRV/RHR trend, device and timestamps
- Garmin readiness components and firmware context
- Session RPE and comparable performance
- Muscle soreness versus focal symptoms
- PTT current/delayed response
- Recent run and strength load
- Fueling context for demanding sessions

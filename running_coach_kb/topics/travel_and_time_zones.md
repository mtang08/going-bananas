# Travel and Time Zones

**Scope.** Air travel with or without a time-zone change, for a recreational runner who also strength trains. Evidence rows E-TRAV-01–34 come from a targeted review verified on 2026-11-22; P029–P041 are reviewable candidates, not activated rules. Heat and altitude need their own reviews. No study enrolled recreational runners who strength train and tested training changes around real flights; most evidence is from small samples of elite male team-sport athletes, plus small triathlete and track-cyclist cohorts that measured sleep and fatigue but not running performance. Treat each rule as a generalization or convention unless it is labeled research.

## Takeaways first

1. **Travel load and circadian load are separate.** A long journey, lost sleep, early wake, and sitting cause fatigue without a clock change; crossing zones adds circadian misalignment [E-TRAV-01; E-TRAV-13] (P029).
2. **Sleep and perceived fatigue change more reliably than measured performance.** Masters triathletes lost about 4.8 h of in-flight sleep, yet reviews found performance decrements in only a minority of studies. No predictable decrement in continuous running is established, so avoid numeric pace penalties [E-TRAV-05; E-TRAV-06].
3. **A red-eye or very early departure is a sleep-loss event even with no zone change.** Acute sleep loss lowered performance by 7.56% on average, with very high heterogeneity [E-TRAV-10; E-TRAV-11] (P032).
4. **Eastward is usually harder and more zones usually worse, but zones explain little individual variation** (models explained 7–18%) [E-TRAV-02; E-TRAV-03].
5. **"One day per zone" estimates clock shift, not readiness.** Sleep settled within about 48 h in some endurance cohorts, some performance measures took three to four days, and some symptoms five to seven or more [E-TRAV-04; E-TRAV-08].
6. **The itinerary sets a provisional plan; actual sleep, symptoms, and warm-up response decide** [E-TRAV-32; E-TRAV-33] (P030).
7. **Cabin dryness and a 3–4% in-flight SpO₂ dip are real, but neither is shown to impair next-day running**, and a flight is not altitude exposure [E-TRAV-14; E-TRAV-15].

## Prior, update, decision

An inference framework, not a validated score.

1. **Prior, from the itinerary.** Set two flags from the snapshot `travel` section. *Circadian load:* trip `zone_shift_hours` and `direction`; below about 3 hours of shift jet lag is usually minor, and 6 or more hours eastward is high. Beyond about 8 hours east, adaptation may proceed by delaying instead, so `direction` is a sign, not a prediction [E-TRAV-02]. *Travel load:* leg `night_overlap`, `duration_hours`, local departure and arrival times, connections, and door-to-door time if reported [E-TRAV-31].
2. **Update, after each travel night.** Sleep and its quality, daytime sleepiness, illness or unusual symptoms, and the response to a warm-up or easy session. Treat unusually high RPE as a same-day readiness signal, not proof of lost fitness [E-TRAV-07]. Missing is unknown, not normal.
3. **Decision, per session.** Protect goal-race and key quality sessions more than optional easy mileage, and hold heavy or technical lifting to a higher readiness bar than mobility or easy running [E-TRAV-34]. Retain, reduce, move, or replace; prefer moving a key session to deleting it.

Precedence: safety symptoms, then sleepiness or illness, then warm-up response, then the itinerary prior [E-TRAV-33].

## Decision rules by situation

### Short daytime flight, 0–2 hours of shift, normal sleep (P031)

**Research.** Five-hour simulated domestic travel had little effect; short-haul rugby travel raised sleepiness and leg tiredness without a clear strength or power loss [E-TRAV-12].

**Inference.** Keep the planned session, with an easier or shorter fallback if delays, stiffness, or unexpected fatigue appear; sitting can leave the legs stiff, so let the warm-up decide [E-TRAV-16]. Not for red-eyes, very early departures, long door-to-door days, a heat or altitude change, or reported poor sleep.

### Red-eye or very early departure, any shift (P032)

**Research.** Two- and four-hour early-morning simulated flights after reduced sleep lowered next-day sprint and jump results by about 3.5–6% in trained footballers [E-TRAV-11].

**Inference.** When sleep was materially curtailed, move hard or long training off the first post-flight day and use rest or easy running. With normal sleep and normal readiness, no fixed cancellation applies. There is no universal hour cutoff; choose training times that do not sacrifice sleep [E-TRAV-19].

### Training on the departure or arrival day (P033)

**Coaching convention.** No trial compares hard training before versus after a flight. Choose the option that preserves sleep, recovery time, and safe logistics. After an overnight or long-haul arrival, default to rest or low-to-moderate activity; hard work needs normal alertness and a normal warm-up. Easy activity serves routine and a readiness check; it is not a jet-lag cure [E-TRAV-17; E-TRAV-18].

### Six or more hours eastward (P034)

**Research.** In an eight-zone crossover, eastward travel produced worse sleep, fatigue, and early intermittent performance than westward [E-TRAV-02]; endurance cohorts' sleep disruption concentrated in the first 48 h [E-TRAV-05; E-TRAV-08].

**Inference.** Provisionally protect the first 24–48 h from the week's highest-value interval, threshold, long, plyometric, or maximal-strength session. Reassess after each local night and extend only while sleep or symptoms stay abnormal. The window is not full adaptation and not a mandatory no-training period.

### Short stays, local time, and light (P035, P036)

**Research.** Keeping home-time sleep reduced layover sleepiness in one study of 19 flight attendants on a 50-hour, nine-zone westward layover, with no benefit after return [E-TRAV-20]. Earlier sleep plus morning bright light advanced circadian phase by about two hours over three days, but symptom and performance benefits of timed light are uncertain [E-TRAV-22].

**Inference.** For stays of about 48 hours or less, home time or partial home time is an option only when local obligations allow. For longer stays, move sleep, meals, light, and training to local time; three days is a pragmatic boundary, not a threshold [E-TRAV-21]. Suggest broadly appropriate destination daylight, but do not improvise precise light timing for large shifts, because mistimed light can work against adaptation. Exercise timing is at most a secondary cue [E-TRAV-23].

### Returning home (P037)

**Research.** Return trips were associated with more fatigue in female national-team footballers, while most measures improved by day two [E-TRAV-24].

**Inference.** Assess the return legs on their own. After a major or sleep-disruptive return (3 or more hours of shift, a red-eye, a very early wake, or a long door-to-door day), place a provisional 24–48 h buffer before a long run or key session, then retain, shorten, or move it based on sleep and warm-up. A short daytime return without a zone change may need no change.

### Goal race across time zones (P038)

**Evidence gap.** No trial establishes an optimal arrival day for an endurance race [E-TRAV-25].

**Coaching convention.** For a goal race across 6 or more hours of shift, avoid arriving inside the first 24–48 h when feasible and target at least three local sleep opportunities. Five to seven nights eastward and three to five westward are conservative conventions, not requirements or guarantees. Keep post-arrival sessions easy or low-cost and race-specific, do not add training to force adaptation, and do not let extra days away disrupt the taper.

### Strength training after travel (P039)

**Research.** After one overnight long-haul trip, reactive jump was about 7% lower on arrival day one and still reduced on day three; there is no robust maximal-strength evidence [E-TRAV-09].

**Inference.** Gate heavy compound, plyometric, or maximal lifting by sleep and readiness as for a hard run, with a normal warm-up and coordination check; after overnight long-haul travel, prefer reduced volume or rescheduling. Easy accessory or mobility work is a different exposure. When `gym_access` is `limited` or `none`, say how the session changes instead of dropping it silently.

### Destination conditions (P040)

**Coaching convention.** Flag heat, altitude, unfamiliar terrain, and unfamiliar treadmills separately. Use the first session to familiarize, and control effort by RPE or heart rate rather than displayed treadmill pace; treadmill and overground running are not interchangeable (treadmill endurance performance SMD −0.50 ± 0.36) [E-TRAV-28]. Heat acclimation is a separate topic that commonly needs repeated exposure over one to two weeks [E-TRAV-26]. A flight is not altitude acclimation; destination altitude needs a separate review, and this guide states no adaptation timeline [E-TRAV-27].

### Long travel and clot safety (P041)

**Clinical guidance.** For flights over four hours, absolute symptomatic VTE risk in healthy travelers is low (about 1 in 4,656–6,000 person-flights), most cases involve other risk factors, and immobility is central [E-TRAV-29].

**Boundary.** Remind the athlete to walk and move the ankles and calves when practical. Refer known risk factors to a clinician before long travel: prior clot, recent surgery, hospitalization, or trauma, active cancer, a cast or major immobility, pregnancy or postpartum, estrogen use, a known clotting disorder or family history, obesity, or older age. Unilateral leg swelling, pain, warmth, or redness, or new breathlessness, chest pain, coughing blood, or fainting after travel needs prompt or urgent medical evaluation [E-TRAV-30]. Do not diagnose, recommend aspirin, anticoagulants, or compression, or claim that hydration prevents clots.

## Inputs mapped to the snapshot

| Input the evidence calls for | Where the coach finds it |
|---|---|
| Signed zone change and direction | `travel.trips[].zone_shift_hours` (positive is east) and `direction`; per leg, `legs[].zone_shift_hours` |
| Local departure and arrival times | `legs[].depart_local` and `legs[].arrive_local`, each with its timezone |
| Flight duration | `legs[].duration_hours`; door-to-door time is not recorded, so ask only when it changes a decision |
| Overlap with usual sleep | `legs[].night_overlap` (the flight crosses 23:00–06:00 in the departure zone): a proxy, not measured sleep |
| Nights away and the return | `nights_away` (null when open-ended); `travel_days` and the final legs |
| Whether the trip is real | `confirmed`; plan around `pending` or `needs_review` trips conditionally |
| Access and availability | `running_access`, `gym_access`, and `days` (an unlisted day is unknown) |
| Heat, altitude, terrain, treadmill | not in the snapshot; ask when it matters for a key session or race |
| Actual sleep, sleepiness, illness, warm-up response | recorded sleep and recovery summaries, check-in `fatigue`, `illness`, symptoms, `context_tags` (`poor_sleep`, `travel`), and session RPE; missing means unknown |

App dates use the home timezone. During a trip, read the athlete's day in `destination_timezone`, and say so when a session date could be ambiguous.

## Prohibited inferences

- "Jet lag means no training for N days" or "recovery takes one day per zone."
- "Under three hours of shift means no travel effect" or "a short flight is harmless": a short red-eye can disrupt more than a longer daytime flight.
- "Eastward is always worse": routes, individuals, and very large shifts differ.
- "Cabin air dehydrates athletes enough to impair performance," "extra water prevents clots," "the SpO₂ dip causes next-day endurance loss," or "a flight counts as altitude exposure."
- "An easy run, a hard workout, or a particular exercise time cures jet lag."
- "Elite male team-sport findings apply directly to recreational runners, women, older athletes, or concurrent run-and-lift training."
- "No symptoms means the body clock has adjusted" or "symptoms prove performance is impaired."
- "A wearable sleep or HRV score alone decides training after travel."
- "Arriving three, five, or seven days early guarantees race readiness" or "hotel-treadmill pace equals outdoor pace."
- Any diagnosis or treatment advice, including melatonin or other medication dosing, supplements, compression, aspirin, or anticoagulants.

## Limitations

Samples are small, mostly elite, male, and team sport. Travel variables explain little individual variation, so the itinerary is a prior, not a forecast. Simulations omit real-flight factors. No trial tests training before versus after a flight, race-arrival buffers, home time beyond a 50-hour westward layover, or hotel treadmills.

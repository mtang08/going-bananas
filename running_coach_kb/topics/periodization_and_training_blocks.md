# Periodization and Training Blocks

## Takeaways first

- Periodization is useful as a planning language, but research does not validate one universal model or a fixed block duration. Definitions and study designs vary substantially [E-PER-02].
- Elite distance-running descriptions commonly show broad sequencing from general preparation toward more race-specific work, with most volume kept low intensity. This is practice evidence with major population and volume mismatch, not a template for a low-volume recreational runner [E-PER-03; E-PER-04].
- For a runner training 3-4 times per week, the main value of an explicit block is **decision compression**: one primary objective, a limited recipe set, stable maintained qualities, and a clear review condition. There is no direct trial showing that naming a block improves outcomes versus a coherent rolling plan [E-PER-05].
- A block should be a reviewable hypothesis. Dates organize the product; completion and early-review conditions determine the coaching decision.
- Separate **sequencing models** (traditional/mixed, block, flexible) from **intensity-distribution descriptions** (pyramidal, polarized, threshold-oriented). They answer different questions.

## What periodization can and cannot establish

### Research-supported or research-informed

- Training response is variable and affected by context; a plan needs checkpoints and adjustment rather than blind adherence to a prewritten sequence [E-PER-02].
- Highly trained runners and successful endurance coaches commonly use broad phases, hard/easy organization, and more competition-specific work as key events approach [E-PER-03; E-PER-04].
- Block periodization may offer small benefits in some trained endurance populations, but the evidence base is small, heterogeneous, and generally low quality [E-PER-01].
- One apparent response or nonresponse is difficult to separate from testing error and within-athlete variation without repeated, comparable observations [E-CAL-04].

### Coaching convention or product heuristic

- Calling a focus a "block" and using a typical 4-8-week window.
- Reviewing the block at a planned checkpoint before its nominal end.
- Committing the next week while leaving much of week two provisional in a rolling 14-day calendar.
- Maintaining one or two qualities with small doses while one primary quality progresses.
- Using a consolidation segment when several demanding changes have accumulated.

These can be sensible without being presented as physiological laws.

## Planning hierarchy

| Layer | Decision question | Required context | Output | Expected stability | Normally preserves | Escalation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Long-term direction | Which goals and seasons matter, and what broad sequence is plausible? | Goal priority, event dates, life constraints, training history | Goal hierarchy and likely future emphases | Months; changes only with a major decision/context shift | Current approved block and calendar until translated | New/cancelled priority event, major availability or health change |
| Training block | What single primary adaptation/tolerance emphasis should constrain choices now? | Previous block, 4-12-week trends, prerequisites, race timing, constraints | Objective, maintained qualities, eligible recipes, review conditions | Several weeks, but trigger-responsive | Long-term goal; approved sessions until accepted | Repeated disruption, objective invalidated, worsening tolerance, race/goal change |
| Rolling 14-day plan | Which concrete session sequence expresses the block under current logistics? | Actual vs planned 7/14 days, current block, recent tolerance/recovery | Specific first-week sessions and provisional second-week intents | Reviewed weekly/cycle | Block objective and maintained qualities | Several failed/missed key exposures or inability to fit the block coherently |
| Daily check-in | What is the safest useful decision for today's session? | Current symptoms/function, recovery, last exposures, today's plan | Proceed/modify/move/rest/clinical input | Hours to days | Block and broader sequence | Repeated modification, pileup risk, or material symptom/context change |

### Why a rolling 14-day horizon can fit

For an alternating four-run/three-run cycle, a calendar-week comparison can misrepresent frequency and session density. Fourteen days captures the full repeating rhythm and both expected long-run/quality exposures. This is a **reasoned product heuristic**, not evidence that physiology operates in 14-day units.

Recommended semantics:

- **Committed:** specific purpose, date range, and session family for the next approximately seven days, subject to daily checks.
- **Provisional:** likely purpose/family for the second week, with dose left contingent on the first week's response.
- **Directional only:** block intent beyond the rolling horizon; do not fabricate daily sessions months ahead.

## Does an explicit block add value?

Use an explicit block when it changes decisions. It is valuable when:

- one objective should narrow eligible recipes;
- progression needs a consistent comparator over several exposures;
- a race or return phase gives a meaningful sequence;
- the coach needs to distinguish maintenance from progression;
- repeated daily/weekly changes otherwise become incoherent; or
- a planned review can determine continue/extend/transition.

Prefer an ongoing goal plus rolling plan when:

- baseline data are too incomplete to define a credible objective;
- the immediate task is simply re-establishing consistent, tolerated running;
- schedule volatility makes multiweek exposure unreliable;
- no decision would differ if a block label were added; or
- clinical uncertainty makes routine progression inappropriate.

The product may create a lightweight "discovery/consolidation" block, but it should not imply a precise adaptation target.

## Sequencing models

### Traditional or mixed sequencing

Multiple qualities remain present while emphasis gradually changes. This resembles common distance-running practice, but "traditional" is heterogeneous. At low run frequency, maintenance elements must be small enough that they do not become several competing quality sessions.

**Use when:** continuity and broad development matter; the runner benefits from one modest quality exposure plus easy/long running.

**Risk:** every maintained quality becomes a progression target, producing too many hard exposures.

### Concentrated or block sequencing

A quality is emphasized more strongly for a short period while others are maintained. Existing meta-analysis suggests possible small benefits in trained endurance athletes, but not enough to require this model [E-PER-01].

**Use when:** prerequisites are stable, the quality benefits from repeated comparable exposures, and recovery capacity supports concentration.

**Risk:** importing high-frequency elite blocks into a 3-4-run schedule; too few total runs may make "concentration" mean sacrificing basic consistency.

### Flexible athlete-responsive sequencing

A sparse plan skeleton is adjusted at checkpoints using execution, tolerance, and context. This is best treated as a planning philosophy rather than a scientifically proven model [E-PER-02].

**Use when:** recovery, symptoms, schedule, and response vary; the system can preserve intent while changing dose or placement.

**Risk:** flexibility without an objective becomes random training. Every change still needs an explicit reason and follow-up.

## Intensity distribution is a separate dimension

Pyramidal, polarized, and threshold-oriented labels describe how intensity is distributed or emphasized. They do not by themselves specify the order of blocks, the day-to-day plan, or the right workout for an individual. Existing E-INT findings remain authoritative:

- no mandatory polarized-versus-pyramidal winner is established;
- zone classification method changes the apparent distribution; and
- threshold-heavy elite systems do not transfer directly to low-volume recreational training.

The active block should select the smallest useful quality dose while protecting aerobic consistency, long-run tolerance, and strength commitments.

## Block definition

A minimum useful block contains:

- **Primary objective:** one capability or tolerance question that can change planning.
- **Baseline:** what is currently known, including uncertainty.
- **Maintained qualities:** what remains present but is not actively progressed.
- **Eligible recipe families:** not an exact calendar.
- **Progression dimension:** normally one major lever at a time.
- **Prerequisites/constraints:** symptoms, function, schedule, strength placement, recent consistency.
- **Completion indicators:** observable evidence, not just an end date.
- **Planned review date:** a product checkpoint.
- **Early-review triggers:** repeated disruption, deteriorating tolerance, goal change, or invalid intensity anchor.
- **Status history:** proposed, approved/active, paused, completed, replaced, ended.

## Lifecycle decisions

### Start

Start only when the objective is narrow, prerequisites are adequate, and the block changes session selection or progression. When baseline is incomplete, start with conservative discovery and explicit missing-data goals rather than false precision.

### Continue

Continue when the objective remains appropriate, exposure is adequate or still accumulating, tolerance is stable, and no wider context has invalidated the plan.

### Extend

Extend when progress is partial or not yet resolved, adherence was adequate enough to justify more exposure, and the same objective remains higher value than transition. Change the flawed element rather than simply adding weeks.

### Consolidate

Consolidate when the objective still matters but recent progression, execution, recovery, or symptom response argues for holding or reducing novelty. Consolidation is responsive, not a mandatory every-third- or fourth-week rule.

### Transition

Transition when the objective is sufficiently achieved, a race-specific need becomes timely, or a different limiting quality now offers greater value. Maintain only what fits the available frequency.

### Replace

Replace when the block's premise is no longer valid: a major interruption, changed goal, new constraint, or discovery that the selected intensity/recipe does not address the actual need.

### End

End when no explicit block is useful, the athlete chooses to stop, routine training should pause, or the objective cannot be pursued responsibly. Ending is not equivalent to success, failure, or permanent nonresponse.

## Low-frequency tradeoffs

With 3-4 runs per week, each run occupies a large share of available exposure. Prioritize in this order unless current goals/constraints say otherwise:

1. Consistent, tolerated aerobic running.
2. A long-run exposure only to the extent it is recovered from.
3. One quality family appropriate to the block, often not every calendar week when tolerance is uncertain.
4. Small maintenance touches such as relaxed strides only when their mechanical cost is acceptable.

Do not combine a new long-run progression, a larger quality dose, and new hills/strides merely because each appears small in isolation.

## Step-back and consolidation periods

Research does not validate a universal every-N-weeks cutback. Use consolidation when:

- several exposures were novel;
- execution quality is deteriorating;
- delayed symptoms are worsening;
- recovery is accumulating poorly;
- schedule/life stress makes the next progression unreliable; or
- a benchmark/race/taper changes the load shape.

A successful, stable week does not require an automatic reduction; a difficult week does not automatically require ending the block.

## Interruption and return

After travel, illness, or a missed sequence, distinguish:

- **brief interruption with preserved tolerance:** resume using a conservative comparable exposure;
- **loss of objective-specific exposure:** rebuild that exposure before testing progression;
- **major interruption or changed function:** replace or restart the block after applying existing return/clinical guidance.

Detraining evidence indicates that complete cessation can reduce endurance adaptations while partial training may mitigate losses, but exact decline and return rates vary [E-TRANS-01]. Do not "make up" missed work.

# Running Evidence Review and Coaching Knowledge-Base Synthesis

**Search date:** 2026-11-10  
**Athlete-context date:** 2026-11-10  
**Review type:** targeted critical literature review; not a registered or exhaustive systematic review  
**Primary artifacts:** topic guides, workflows, evidence matrix, source registry, and reviewable policy candidates in this package

## A. Decision summary

1. **Build continuity around a rolling 14-day cycle, not a mandatory weekly percentage.** The 10% rule did not reduce injury in a novice-runner trial, and prospective studies do not establish one safe progression threshold [E-AER-01–02]. **Certainty:** moderate for rejecting a universal rule. **Applicability:** high. **Practical consequence:** preserve the agreed rolling-cycle run count and progress one primary running dimension only after a tolerated cycle.

2. **Use individual-run novelty as a warning, not a safe/unsafe line.** A 5,205-runner Garmin cohort associated sessions more than 10% longer than the recent longest run with higher overuse-injury rates, but the finding is observational and not PTT-specific [E-AER-03]. **Certainty:** moderate for association, low for causality/cutoff. **Applicability:** high as a dashboard flag. **Consequence:** compare planned long runs with recent comparable exposures and review delayed symptoms; do not “clear” a run just below a boundary.

3. **Do not operationalize ACWR, monotony, strain, or long-run percentage caps as injury gates.** ACWR has conceptual/statistical problems and inconsistent running evidence; no validated long-run cap was found [E-AER-04, E-LONG-02]. **Certainty:** moderate for rejecting causal ACWR use; insufficient for a universal cap. **Applicability:** high. **Consequence:** display descriptively, if at all, and never override symptoms.

4. **Return after illness in stages.** First exercise, full training, and full performance are different milestones [E-AER-05–06]. **Certainty:** moderate clinical guidance/low–moderate cohort evidence. **Applicability:** high for uncomplicated respiratory illness. **Consequence:** first session is a controlled easy challenge; monitor through the next day; do not repay missed workouts.

5. **Use predominantly easy/pyramidal training as a practical pattern, not an 80/20 command.** Polarized and pyramidal approaches both improve endurance, with no stable overall winner and possible recreational advantage for pyramidal training [E-INT-01–03]. **Certainty:** moderate. **Applicability:** high. **Consequence:** retain one controlled quality run and one controlled long run per week only when both, plus lifting, are tolerated.

6. **Do not import elite Norwegian threshold doses or ambiguous “Zone 2” labels.** Double-threshold evidence is descriptive/acute and elite; zone systems are incompatible [E-INT-04–06]. **Certainty:** high for classification problem, low for method superiority. **Applicability:** very high. **Consequence:** record the zone model and use RPE/talk-test plus validated anchors; one controlled threshold session is adequate when appropriate.

7. **Treat PTT-region symptoms and delayed function as the primary running constraint.** PTT-specific trials are small and do not validate loading or return-to-run cutoffs; Achilles pain numbers do not transfer [E-PTT-01–07]. **Certainty:** low for exact treatment, high for diagnostic limits. **Applicability:** very high. **Consequence:** leave numeric pain thresholds null, track 0–2-hour/next-morning/later response, and seek clinical input for progressive or function-limiting patterns.

8. **Preserve meaningful strength training as a coequal goal when it is part of the current plan.** Concurrent training generally preserves maximal strength and hypertrophy; explosive outcomes are more vulnerable, especially same-session [E-HYB-01, E-HYB-04]. **Certainty:** moderate. **Applicability:** high. **Consequence:** adjust order, separation, lower-body dose, novelty, and proximity to failure before deleting strength.

9. **Use multi-signal recovery reasoning.** One ordinary poor night should not automatically cancel an easy run; several agreeing days of fatigue, sleep disruption, resting-physiology change, or poor performance warrant modification [E-REC-01–04]. **Certainty:** low–moderate. **Applicability:** high. **Consequence:** symptoms and a short subjective check-in outrank one HRV/readiness score; use warm-up response and trends.

10. **Use Garmin for trends with quality controls, not precise prediction.** Wrist HR, HRV, sleep staging, VO2max, race prediction, and readiness all have device/algorithm limits [E-WEAR-01–05]. **Certainty:** low–moderate for device validity; insufficient for readiness benefit. **Applicability:** high. **Consequence:** expose component data and confounders, compare standardized sessions, and never let a favorable score clear worsening symptoms.

11. **Support demanding training with simple fueling practices without calorie surveillance.** Carbohydrate availability matters more for quality/long sessions, and regular protein supports resistance adaptation; REDs cannot be diagnosed from Garmin calories or fatigue [E-FUEL-01–03]. **Certainty:** moderate for general principles. **Applicability:** high. **Consequence:** use brief preparedness prompts, optional food-based experiments, and qualified referral for persistent concerns.

## C. Detailed synthesis

### 1. Aerobic development and progression

The evidence does not identify a universal safe weekly increase. The 10% trial directly failed to reduce injuries, while the broader prospective literature is heterogeneous and conflicting [E-AER-01–02]. This should change the app’s logic: percent change is context, not clearance. Recent training, individual-run size, terrain, speed, strength work, symptoms, and illness all affect the interpretation.

The strongest recent running-specific signal is the single-session finding in the 5,205-runner Garmin cohort [E-AER-03]. Its effect estimates are meaningful enough to surface novelty, but association is not causation. The app should show the planned run against the recent longest **comparable** run and explain when route, pace, or data gaps undermine the comparison. It must not label 9% safe and 11% unsafe.

ACWR should not become an automated coaching target. The ratio can create artifacts and implies causal control that the data do not support [E-AER-04]. Monotony and strain have similar limitations as predictive gates. The useful replacement is a transparent 14-day load shape: individual-run sizes, demanding-exposure clustering, missing sessions, and symptom responses.

Return after illness differs from uninterrupted progression. IOC guidance supports a controlled exercise challenge for selected mild respiratory illness only after symptoms are very mild/resolved and without concerning features [E-AER-05]. A 2026 cohort shows why first training cannot be equated with full performance [E-AER-06]. The practical implication is staged restoration—easy exposure, next-day review, then gradual reintroduction of duration and quality. Prior higher weekly volume is historical capacity, not proof of current readiness.

Time and distance should both be retained. Duration is often more portable across pace and terrain; distance adds mechanical context. Frequency is a user preference—the agreed rolling-cycle run count—so the knowledge base progresses within that pattern. Cutback weeks are a coaching tool, but no validated fixed cadence was found; use consolidation when the prior cycle was novel, symptomatic, poorly recovered, or difficult to interpret.

**App implication:** use P004, P006–P008, P013–P016 and `topics/aerobic_progression.md`.

### 2. Intensity distribution and session mix

Polarized, pyramidal, threshold-oriented, and predominantly easy approaches are broad descriptions, not interchangeable recipes. The two major syntheses conflict slightly: one reports a small VO2peak advantage for polarized training, while the individual-participant analysis finds no overall polarized-versus-pyramidal difference and possible recreational benefit for pyramidal training [E-INT-02–03]. The small recreational trial also failed to show a significant primary between-group difference [E-INT-01]. This supports method choice by phase and tolerance rather than ideology.

Counting sessions and counting time in zones answer different questions. Warm-ups, recoveries, HR lag, and incompatible two-/three-/five-zone definitions can make the same plan appear polarized or not [E-INT-04]. The app should store the session goal and raw anchors, name the zone model, and avoid a distribution target unless the denominator and method are explicit.

At the agreed rolling-cycle run count, one quality session and one long run each week can make sense, but only if the long run remains controlled and the combined mechanical/physiological cost—including lifting—is tolerated. A long run is not “easy” merely because it is below threshold. During the rebuild, a second formal quality day is not justified by polarized or 80/20 logic.

Controlled threshold intervals are the most plausible current quality method once symptoms are stable because duration and effort can be bounded. Multi-minute VO2 work is a later, phase-specific tool for mile/5K goals. Relaxed strides can preserve speed familiarity, but direct minimum-dose and PTT-safety evidence is insufficient [E-INT-08]. Hills, hill sprints, and all-out sprint intervals create distinct plantarflexor/foot and acceleration demands.

Norwegian double threshold is not a default. Elite best-practice descriptions and an acute split-session study do not prove long-term superiority, and their lactate control, volume, and recovery resources do not transfer [E-INT-06]. Likewise, “Zone 2” must be defined; an expert viewpoint supports work around/below the first threshold but notes adaptations are not exclusive to a narrow band [E-INT-05].

**App implication:** use P009–P010, P014–P015 and `topics/intensity_and_workout_methods.md`; method details are in `references/method_comparison.md`.

### 3. Long-run development and marathon foundations

Longer endurance runs and higher weekly volume are associated with half/marathon performance, but observational evidence cannot select a safe personal dose [E-LONG-01]. Popular long-run percentage caps are coaching conventions, not validated injury-prevention thresholds [E-LONG-02]. At approximately a lower rebuilding volume, the long run’s share may look large; the useful question is whether it is novel, controlled, and tolerated—not whether it crosses a universal percentage.

A reported long run with an observed pace lacks effort, HR quality, terrain, weather, and fueling context. It should be recorded as a substantial exposure and classified as unknown intensity. It cannot establish easy pace or a half-marathon prediction.

Long-run progression should follow stable shorter runs and known delayed symptom response. When duration increases, hold intensity, terrain, and formal quality broadly stable. Progress over 1–2 years should emphasize continuity, distributed aerobic volume, strength, fueling skills, and race experience, not a premature marathon block. Mile/5K phases can rotate threshold, VO2, and speed emphasis while maintaining easy volume and a tolerable long run.

Cycling and other low-impact modes can preserve/add aerobic work when running is constrained, but the evidence is short and heterogeneous; no one-for-one conversion preserves running tissue tolerance [E-LONG-03].

**App implication:** use P006–P010, P014–P017 and `topics/long_runs_and_race_development.md`.

### 4. Strength, mobility, and scheduling

Concurrent training generally preserves hypertrophy and maximal strength relative to resistance training alone, while explosive strength is more susceptible to interference and same-session fatigue [E-HYB-01]. This supports the athlete’s identity as a hybrid athlete and the preference for two meaningful full-body sessions.

High-load, plyometric, and combined strength methods can improve running economy, but economy is a surrogate and not equivalent to race performance or injury prevention [E-HYB-02]. Running-injury prevention evidence is mixed; one supervised hip/core trial was favorable, while pooled exercise programs were not clearly protective [E-HYB-03]. The coach should never sell strength or mobility as insurance.

Scheduling should be individualized around priority, soreness, and actual session quality. When same-day consolidation is used, perform the current priority first and separate sessions when practical. A subgroup suggesting less explosive-strength interference with at least several hours’ separation does not establish a universal three-hour rule [E-HYB-04]. Preferred strength days should remain the default; alternate days are an option when the actual two-week run cycle benefits.

Calf/soleus/inversion work and plyometrics count as lower-leg load. Heavy resistance can remain meaningful while volume, novelty, or proximity to failure are adjusted near quality/long runs. A current mobility session can be retained for comfort and task-specific needs without assuming more flexibility prevents injury.

**App implication:** use P009, P011–P012, P014, P017 and `topics/hybrid_strength_and_scheduling.md`.

### 5. PTT symptoms and tendon load management

The app must preserve diagnostic boundaries. Posterior-tibial-region tenderness does not establish tendinopathy, PTTD, or PCFD, and flat feet alone do not diagnose deformity [E-PTT-04]. PTT-specific reviews and two small trials support conservative exercise packages but do not establish an optimal mode, load, frequency, pain cutoff, or return-to-running test [E-PTT-01–02].

Broader tendinopathy evidence favors adequately loaded progressive resistance and not necessarily daily loading, but PTT was excluded [E-PTT-03]. The correct use is to inform clinician questions, not generate a heavy-slow-resistance prescription. Isometrics may remain if an individual finds them helpful, but they are not consistently superior in other tendons [E-PTT-07]. Short-foot work can remain if comfortable; current evidence concerns surrogate foot outcomes rather than validated PTT running return [E-PTT-05]. Orthoses may reduce pain in diagnosed early-stage PTTD, but they are a clinician-guided load modifier, not an app-selected cure.

Delayed response is the most important operational signal. When an athlete reports worse tenderness later after longer runs, monitoring should include before, during, 0–2 hours after, next morning, and the later window. An Achilles pain-monitoring trial supports the concept of tracked activity but its numeric cutoffs do not transfer [E-PTT-06]. All numeric PTT thresholds remain null.

Clinical input matters when symptoms progress, function/walking deteriorates, swelling/weakness/shape change or trauma occurs, or repeated load modification fails. The app must state that it cannot diagnose.

**App implication:** use P005–P006, P008–P009, P011–P012, P018 and `topics/ptt_and_load_tolerance.md`.

### 6. Recovery and daily adaptation

Severe sleep loss impairs performance on average, but evidence is less certain for one ordinary suboptimal night [E-REC-01]. The desired behavior—encouraging adherence—fits the evidence: an easy run can usually proceed when symptoms, illness, and multi-day recovery are normal, with optional ambition removed. Demanding sessions should have an easy fallback based on warm-up response.

Subjective monitoring deserves equal status to device data. One small trial favored subjective-guided training, but its sample and duration make certainty low [E-REC-02]. A brief check-in can still capture tendon response, illness, fatigue, and soreness that Garmin cannot.

HRV-guided training may reduce hard-session count and modestly improve some outcomes, but pooled superiority is small/inconsistent and study protocols differ from overnight Garmin metrics [E-REC-03]. Use same-device multi-day trends and agreement with RHR, sleep, fatigue, and performance. A favorable HRV or readiness score is never injury clearance.

A 2026 readiness review finds no stable universal algorithm [E-REC-04]. Therefore the agent should modify the next demanding exposure when several signals agree, investigate illness/training/life stress/fueling, and avoid diagnosing overtraining.

**App implication:** use P001–P005, P012, P015, P017 and `topics/recovery_and_daily_adaptation.md`.

### 7. Garmin and measurable progress

Independent validation supports trend use but exposes systematic error. In a small Forerunner 265 study, VO2max was overestimated and half-marathon predictions were optimistic [E-WEAR-01]. A sleep study on another Garmin model found much better sleep/wake than sleep-stage agreement [E-WEAR-02]. Nocturnal HRV showed reasonable concordance in a small different-model study but meaningful individual error [E-WEAR-03]. Older-model wrist-HR validation shows useful steady-running correlation with latency/artifact limits [E-WEAR-04].

Garmin states that Training Readiness combines sleep, recovery time, HRV status, acute load, sleep history, and stress. That accurately describes the product, not an independently validated coaching benefit [E-WEAR-05]. The dashboard should show components, timestamps, missingness, and conflicts.

Best progress indicators are adherence, symptom-stable consistency, more aerobic work at comparable effort, stable delayed response, quality-session control, and preserved strength. Pace/HR and decoupling become meaningful only under comparable route, elevation, surface, weather, sensor, effort, fueling, and fatigue. Session RPE is practical context but not an injury predictor [E-DATA-01–02].

**App implication:** use P001–P003, P005, P010, P015 and `topics/wearables_and_metric_interpretation.md`.

### 8. Fueling for concurrent training

Problematic low energy availability can harm health and performance, but REDs is a clinical, multifactorial syndrome. Fatigue or Garmin calories cannot diagnose it [E-FUEL-01]. The app should treat inadequate fueling as one possible confounder and refer when persistent fatigue combines with health, injury, dietary, or weight concerns.

Carbohydrate availability should scale with training demand [E-FUEL-02]. Short easy runs often require no special protocol; quality, longer, and double-session days merit deliberate preparation and optional during-session practice as duration grows. Exact grams depend on body size, total diet, GI tolerance, goals, and duration, so the package leaves them unspecified.

Protein supports resistance adaptation, with small average supplementation benefits when total intake is lower [E-FUEL-03]. The practical default is regular protein-containing meals and recovery food, not mandatory supplements or tracking. Hybrid scheduling and fueling should be reviewed together when the second session repeatedly underperforms.

**App implication:** use P003, P010–P012, P017–P018 and `topics/fueling_and_energy_availability.md`.

## E. Minimum data specification

The design target is a 10–20-second daily check-in. Missing observations are stored as `null` with `observed_at` and `quality_status`; they are never imputed as normal. Delayed symptoms must link back to the likely provoking session while retaining attribution uncertainty.

| Field | Source | Cadence | Required/optional | Use | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Planned session purpose/structure | App/plan | Per session | Required | Define decision and preserve intent | Plan may be stale after changes |
| Completed activity timestamp, mode, duration, distance | Garmin/app | Per session | Required when available | Adherence, volume, run novelty | Export availability must be verified |
| Route/elevation/surface tag | Garmin + derived/manual | Per run | Optional but high value | Comparability and mechanical context | Surface often unavailable automatically |
| Pace/splits | Garmin | Per run | Optional for decisions | Standardized progress and execution | Heat, hills, GPS, stops |

| PTT delayed response | Manual | Next morning and/or later key run | Required when relevant | Determine tolerance/progression | Attribution confounded by lifting/daily activity |

## G. Unknowns and research gaps

### Questions the athlete must answer

1. What does the actual recent 6–12-week running export show?
2. What is the repeatable delayed symptom/function response after specific run types?
3. What lower-body lift content and soreness windows matter most?
4. Is the half marathon confirmed, and when?
5. What exact strength/hypertrophy priorities should influence session order?
6. Which minimal subjective fields feel sustainable in a 10–20-second check-in?

### Information imported data can resolve

- Current volume/frequency and illness interruption
- Long-run size and run-to-run novelty
- Quality/long-run/strength placement
- Pace/HR trends under tagged conditions
- Missingness/plausibility of Garmin fields
- Adherence and outcomes of prior recommendations

### Questions for a clinician or sports dietitian

- Diagnosis/stage and relevant functional findings for the posterior-tibial-region symptoms
- Individual load, orthosis, exercise, and return-to-running criteria
- Need for imaging/referral when function, swelling, weakness, or shape changes are present
- Nutrition/REDs assessment if persistent fatigue or health signs emerge

### Literature cannot currently resolve

- PTT-specific pain/return-to-run thresholds and optimal dose
- A validated personalized running-injury predictor from wearable load
- The best progression strategy for low-volume post-illness recreational runners
- The ideal distribution at the agreed rolling-cycle run count with two meaningful lifts
- A validated minimum stride/hill dose for tendon-limited runners
- A universal long-run cap
- The best exact run–lift schedule for simultaneous endurance, hypertrophy, maximal, and explosive goals
- Whether Garmin Training Readiness improves outcomes
- Stable current-firmware validity for every wearable metric
- A low-burden, high-specificity field screen for problematic low energy availability

## H. Search and source appendix

- **Search date:** 2026-11-10
- **Approach:** targeted searches of PubMed/PMC, publisher pages, professional consensus/guidelines, device-validation literature, and official Garmin documentation for product definitions
- **Selection priorities:** systematic reviews/meta-analyses, trials, prospective cohorts, consensus/clinical guidance, then device/methods studies; recreational populations preferred; elite practice explicitly downgraded for applicability
- **Major terms:** running progression/injury, 10% rule, ACWR, single-session distance spike, illness return, polarized/pyramidal/threshold/Zone 2, Norwegian threshold, intervals/strides/hills, long run/cross-training, concurrent strength, running economy/injury prevention, posterior tibial tendon/PTTD/PCFD, orthoses/intrinsic foot/isometrics, sleep/HRV/readiness, Garmin validation, session RPE/decoupling, REDs/carbohydrate/protein, block periodization
- **Access limitations:** several sources were abstract-only; these are labeled. Device studies often use older/different Garmin models and small samples. Manufacturer documentation describes claims, not benefit.
- **No snippet evidence:** search snippets were not used as evidence.
